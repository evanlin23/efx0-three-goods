## 1. Definitions (as in k4/sdc.md §29 and its code)
- **Setting.**
  - Agents have additive values v_i(g) ≥ 0, with R_i = {g : v_i(g) > 0}.
  - An order π gives serial-dictatorship picks y_i: each agent in turn takes its favourite remaining good of R_i, ties
    to the smaller index, and nothing if none is left.
  - J is the set of unpicked goods. Every junk good is worth at most y_i to each agent i (k4/sdc.md §2, (I2)).
- **Chain** (k4/sdc/de_free.py `moves`, `CHAIN_ONLY`). Distinct agents x₀, …, x_t; the agents x₀, …, x_{t−1} pass
  nonempty bases.
  - x₀ takes H₀ ⊆ J, plus goods of B_{x_t} when t ≥ 1. When t = 0, H₀ is *added* to x₀'s pick.
  - Each x_i with i ≥ 1 takes B_{x_{i−1}} ∪ H_i, with H_i ⊆ J.
  - The sets H are disjoint, inside their receivers' R, and every agent on the chain strictly gains. The rest of
    B_{x_t} becomes junk.
- **One-absorber completion of bases B.** A complete EFX₀ allocation X ⊇ B in which at most one agent receives two or
  more goods outside its base.
- **OC₁(t ≥ 0)**, as tested by `k4/sdc/oc_absorb.py`: for every order, the picks have a one-absorber completion, or
  the bases after one chain with t ≥ 0 have one.
- **OC₁(t ≥ 1)**: the same with t ≥ 1, as written in §29.
- **Other classes.**
  - OC: free completion, t ≥ 1.
  - MD: some EFX₀ allocation value-dominates the picks.
  - SD-C: some order's picks extend under the free completion.

## 2. Lemmas (any n, any k; written proofs)

**Lemma R (reachability).** Fix π, its picks y and junk J, and an EFX₀ allocation X. X is a one-absorber completion
of the bases after some chain with path x₀, …, x_t iff, with
H*_i = X_{x_i} ∩ J ∩ R_{x_i} for i ≥ 1 and H*₀ = X_{x₀} ∩ (J ∪ B_{x_t}) ∩ R_{x₀} (for t = 0: X_{x₀} ∩ J ∩ R_{x₀}),
all of the following hold:
1. *Placement.*
   - For t ≥ 1: y_{x_{i−1}} ∈ X_{x_i} for 1 ≤ i ≤ t, and y_a ∈ X_a for every agent a off the path.
   - For t = 0: y_a ∈ X_a for every a, and H*₀ ≠ ∅.
2. *Gains.*
   - For t ≥ 1: v_{x_i}(y_{x_{i−1}}) + v_{x_i}(H*_i) > v_{x_i}(y_{x_i}) for i ≥ 1, and v_{x₀}(H*₀) > v_{x₀}(y_{x₀}).
   - Missing picks count 0. The passed picks y_{x₀}, …, y_{x_{t−1}} exist.
3. *Absorber.* At most one agent a has |X_a ∖ B*_a| ≥ 2. Here B*_{x₀} = H*₀ (y_{x₀} ∪ H*₀ for t = 0),
   B*_{x_i} = {y_{x_{i−1}}} ∪ H*_i, and B*_a = {y_a} off the path.

*Proof.* (⇐) The sets H* define a valid chain:
- they are disjoint, since they lie in different bundles;
- they lie inside J, or J ∪ B_{x_t} for H*₀, and inside the receivers' R;
- the passed bases lie in the receivers' bundles by condition 1;
- the gains hold by condition 2.

Its bases B* are subsets of X, and condition 3 is the absorber condition for X.

(⇒) Let a chain with sets H_i and a one-absorber completion X be given. Since bases are subsets of final bundles,
H_i ⊆ X_{x_i} ∩ J ∩ R_{x_i} = H*_i, and likewise H₀ ⊆ H*₀. The passed picks lie in the receivers' bundles, which gives
condition 1. Replacing each H_i by H*_i only adds positively valued goods to a base, so the gains of condition 2 still
hold. The goods outside the bases only shrink, X_a ∖ B*_a ⊆ X_a ∖ B_a, which gives condition 3. ∎

So OC₁(t ≥ 0) for π says: some EFX₀ allocation satisfies conditions 1–3 for some path, or keeps all picks with one
absorber. OC₁(t ≥ 1) is the same without t = 0. This is what `kgen/oc3.c` and `kgen/ocn.c` test, and
`kgen/verify_oc1.py`, which enumerates every chain and every choice of the sets H explicitly, agrees on every
counterexample.

**Lemma P (peeling a top-heavy agent).** Suppose some agent z has a pick y_z with v_z(y_z) ≥ v_z(M ∖ {y_z}). If OC₁
holds for the order π − z on the instance I − z − y_z (either form), it holds for π on I (same form).

*Proof.*
1. *The other picks are unchanged.*
   - An agent before z in π did not take y_z, so removing y_z does not change its favourite.
   - An agent after z never saw y_z.

   So the junk is the same.
2. *Add {y_z} as z's bundle to the witness X′ for I − z − y_z.*
   - z does not strongly envy anyone: v_z(X′_j ∖ g) ≤ v_z(M ∖ y_z) ≤ v_z(y_z).
   - Nobody strongly envies a singleton.
   - The bundles of X′ are unchanged, so EFX₀ among the other agents still holds.
3. *The move still qualifies.* z is off the chain, keeps its pick and receives no other good. A chain of the
   sub-instance is a chain of I, because its sets lie in the same junk. The absorber count is unchanged. ∎

This is rule R1 of PROMPT.md L2 in OC₁ form. It reduces OC₁ at n = 3 to n = 2 whenever some agent's pick is worth as
much to it as all other goods together.

**Lemma S (picks are lost only as singletons).** Let X be EFX₀, and let g be a good in another agent's bundle X_j with
|X_j| ≥ 2. Then v_i(X_i) ≥ v_i(g) for every agent i ≠ j. So if v_i(X_i) < v_i(y_i), the pick y_i, and every good that i
values above X_i, forms a singleton bundle of another agent.

*Proof.* Take h ∈ X_j ∖ {g}. Then v_i(X_i) ≥ v_i(X_j ∖ h) ≥ v_i(g). ∎

So MD fails for π only through picks sitting alone in other bundles. With n = 3 there are at most two such
singletons per agent.

## 4. What repairs the hard starts
- **n = 3** (`kgen/shapes3.py`, 2,000 random instances, m = 6–11, 2,390 hard starts):
  - **T1Z** repairs every hard start: a t = 1 chain (one hand-off x₀ → x₁; the third agent z keeps its pick) in which
    z is the absorber or nobody absorbs.
  - No fixed role works: z = the first agent covers 2,286 starts, z = the last agent 2,320, and x₀ frozen 1,377. At
    large k the chain is driven by the amount of junk, not by who wants whose pick.
  - On the counterexamples to OC₁(t ≥ 1) no t = 1 chain repairs, but a t = 0 move does: a second agent absorbs goods
    it values.
  - The agent of that move and the absorber take all six combinations of order positions over 61 failing starts.
- **n = 2** (`kgen/shapes2.py`, 600 instances, 1,200 starts). With a the first picker and b the second, "A1 or B"
  covers every start:
  - **A1:** both keep their picks, and a holds at most one good it values at 0.
  - **B:** a hands y_a to b; a gets goods worth more than y_a; b's bundle without y_b is worth more than y_b to b.

  So with two agents only the first picker ever has to move. For comparison:
  - "A1 or A2" (keep both picks; either agent holds at most one worthless good) covers 1,170 starts;
  - "B or C" (one hand-off, in either direction) covers 767.

## 5. PD (P1) at n = 3: the Chaudhury–Garg–Mehlhorn instance
- **Source.** arXiv 2002.05119v3, §5, Theorem 21, Table 3. Read verbatim in the HTML by a web-fetch subagent; table
  cells are doubled in that rendering and were resolved by the proof's own arithmetic.
- **The instance.** Values of goods g1–g7:
  - agent 1: (8, 2, 12, 2, 0, 17, 1);
  - agent 2: (5, 0, 9, 4, 10, 0, 3);
  - agent 3: (0, 0, 0, 0, 9, 10, 2).
- **The partial allocation.** X = ({g2, g3, g4}, {g1, g5}, {g6}), with g7 unallocated, is partial EFX₀ with values
  (16, 15, 10), and no complete EFX₀ allocation reaches them. The paper's progress measure is accordingly a
  lexicographic potential (v_a(X_a), v_b(X_b), v_c(X_c)), with a, b, c named arbitrarily (§2 there), not Pareto
  domination.
- **Replay here.**
  - `kgen/pd3.c` finds exactly this one undominated partial EFX₀ allocation among 740.
  - The pure-Python check `pd3_hunt.verify` confirms it.
  - `kgen/pd3_shrink.py` shrinks it to agent 1 (5, 1, 12, 1, 0, 15, 0), the others unchanged: k = 5, the same X.
- **On this instance** OC₁ (both forms) and MD hold from every order.
- **What random testing showed.** The random PD hunt (4,000 instances, `pd3_hunt_2_4000_5_11.log`) and 120 fixed-m
  climbs (`pd3_climb_fixm_11_120_800_6_9.log`) found nothing. It is one more case where random tests miss a real
  failure.
- **Consequences.**
  - Value-monotone completion fails from n = 3 on, at k = 5. At n = 4 and k = 4 it fails too (K4.SDC.P1). At n = 3
    and k = 4 it is untested adversarially.
  - MD and PD separate at n = 3.

## 6. Where a proof would have to work (no n = 3 proof yet)
- **The n = 3 target.** OC₁(t ≥ 0). By Lemma R it is a statement about EFX₀ allocations with prescribed pick
  positions. In the all-positive case it becomes one of three shapes:
  1. every agent keeps its pick and some agent holds at most two goods (a t = 0 move plus the absorber);
  2. T1Z: z keeps y_z, x₁ holds y_{x₀}, and x₀ and x₁ gain strictly (x₁ not counting y_{x₁});
  3. a rotation (t = 2).
- **Large k.** The one-absorber picks fail as soon as there is much junk, so a chain, or a t = 0 move, is needed on
  most starts. The proof is then a proof of EFX₀ existence for three agents with these placement constraints.
- **What is not available.**
  - No value-monotone method can work (§5).
  - The known existence proofs, CGM's lexicographic potential and Akrami et al.'s potential over partitions
    (arXiv 2205.07638, §4, read pp. 9–12), do not track where the picks end up.
- **What uses n ≤ 3, as far as the data show.**
  - *One chain suffices.* At n = 4, two hand-offs on disjoint pairs of agents can be needed (§7), and one chain cannot
    provide both. With three agents there are no two disjoint pairs.
  - So a general-n statement must allow several disjoint chains: OCP (§7) or a fixed number of chains. A proof of
    OC₁ at n = 3 would have to use, at some step, that any two hand-offs share an agent.
  - *At two agents* (§8): cut-and-choose, the envy-freeness of the chooser, and Fact L on 2-partitions. None of these
    is available at n = 3 as stated.
- **What uses k.** One chain fails at n = 4 only from k = 5 on (§7), and the t ≥ 1 form fails at n = 3 from k = 5
  (§3). In both counterexamples, junk-rich agents value their pick, several junk goods, and a good they end up
  with, which uses five goods. A k = 4 proof of OC₁ must use that agents cannot value that many goods.
- **Next step.**
  - Close the two-agent open case (§8).
  - Then Lemma P for n = 3.
  - Then a T1Z construction in which the absorber z is chosen last, as Lemma K chooses LB⁺'s owner.

  - 160 climbs with m = 7–10 (`climb_n4_code_k4_31_160_1500_7_10.log`): no counterexample.
  - 40 climbs started from the strict k = 5 counterexample, with goods valued beyond 4 per agent penalized first
    (`climb_n4_code_seeded_k4_41_40_1500.log`): no counterexample.
  - A caveat on what this shows. 120 climbs capped at k ≤ 5 from random starts, n = 4, m = 7–10
    (`climb_n4_code_k5_32_120_1500_7_10.log`), found no counterexample either, although k = 5 counterexamples exist.
    They came only from uncapped climbs followed by shrinking. So the k ≤ 4 results are weak evidence.
  - n = 5, k ≤ 4, strict values, m = 8–9 (`climb_n5_code_k4_53_100_800_8_9.log`): 100 rounds, no counterexample. Many
    end with a single reachable EFX₀ allocation.
  - MD at n = 4, no k cap, m = 7–9 (`climb_n4_md_81_100_800_7_9.log`): 100 rounds, no counterexample, down to a single
    dominating allocation.
  - SD-C at n = 4, same setting (`climb_n4_sdc_82_100_800_7_9.log`): 100 rounds, no counterexample, down to one
    order with a single free completion.
  - n = 5 uncapped, m = 8–9 (`climb_n5_code_61_100_1000_8_9.log`): stopped at 76 of 100 rounds, with one
    counterexample. Shrunk with k lowered first and ties allowed (`shrink_n5_a.log`), it gets only to m = 9, k = 6,
    failing from 7 orders. That is no lower than the k = 5 counterexamples of n = 4 here and of PW5 (n = 5) in
    k4/ksens.md.
  - OCP at n = 4, no k cap, m = 7–10 (`climb_n4_pack_71_120_1000_7_10.log`): 120 rounds, no counterexample. OCP, a
    round of vertex-disjoint chains, is also the k-sensitivity session's "round" conjecture, which they own.
  - The k-sensitivity session (`claude/k4-ksens`, `k4/ksens.md` §1) independently refutes OC and OC₁ at k = 5. Its
    PW5 instance has n = 5, m = 9, and a written proof. Three frozen agents share a pool in which every pair is a
    danger set, and two wanters keep them frozen. With three agents there are at most two frozen agents and one
    wanter, so PW cannot occur at n = 3. This matches the two-disjoint-pairs heuristic of §6.
- **The two-pair mechanism** (two-chain repairs listed by a BFS over `kgen/verify_oc1_n.py`'s chain states).
  - Every two-chain repair found uses two hand-offs on *disjoint pairs*: agent 0 takes junk {0, 5} and passes its pick
    to agent 2, and agent 3 takes junk and passes its pick to agent 1. Example: X = ({0, 5, 6}, {1, 7}, {4}, {2, 3}).
  - One chain must link the two hand-offs into a path. Then an agent receives a pick it does not value and has to
    take junk that the other junk-rich agent values, and that agent strongly envies it.
  - At n = 3 there is no room for two disjoint pairs, which is a heuristic reason why one chain suffices there on
    every instance tested.
  - On this instance, each junk-rich agent values its pick, three junk goods (two of them shared) and the pick it
    ends up with: five goods. That may be why k ≤ 4 resists.
- **OCP: a packing of chains** (`kgen/ocn.c`, class PACK). Several vertex-disjoint chains are applied together, each
  with its sets inside the original junk, the other agents keep their picks, and then a one-absorber completion
  follows. OCP implies MD for the order, since every chain agent gains, and so TARGET.
  - OCP holds from every order on all seven n = 4 counterexamples.
  - It is permissive: 125 of the 127 EFX₀ allocations qualify on the strict instance.
  - Climbs against OCP at n = 4 are queued.
- **Reading.** OC and OC₁ are k = 4 statements, or at least k ≤ 4 statements, at n = 4. A general-k version needs
  more chains, two on the data, possibly one per pair of agents. The order-free conjecture MD and the existential
  SD-C still survive at n = 4 and every k.

## 8. Two agents: what is proved (positive generic values)
*Superseded by §9*, which proves the two-agent theorem for any values, with the open case below handled by
removing both tops before cutting. This section is kept as the record of how that case was found.

**Setting.**
- Two agents a (the first picker) and b. y_a is a's top good, and y_b is b's top good in M ∖ {y_a}.
- Values are positive and *generic*: no agent values two different sets equally.
- So every one-absorber condition is automatic, and the shapes of §4 read:
  - (A1) y_a ∈ X_a and y_b ∈ X_b;
  - (B) y_a ∈ X_b, v_a(X_a) > v_a(y_a), and v_b(X_b ∖ {y_b}) > v_b(y_b).
- Both are OC₁(t ≥ 0) shapes (t = 0 at a, and a t = 1 chain a → b).

**LPT_c.** Goods in decreasing v_c order, each to the bundle of smaller current v_c value (the first bundle on a tie).

**Fact L** (classical; proof for completeness). The LPT_c partition (P, Q) is EFX for c, and it puts c's two most
valued goods in different bundles.
- *Proof.* Goods arrive in decreasing order, so the last good h added to a bundle Z is its minimum. When h was added,
  Z ∖ h was not richer than the other bundle, which only grew afterwards. So v_c(Z) − v_c(h) ≤ v_c(other). The first
  good goes to P, and the second to the then empty Q. ∎

**Lemma 2 (two agents).** Shape A1 or B is attained by cut-and-choose:
- **(i)** if v_b(y_a) > v_b(y_b): b cuts by LPT_b and a chooses;
- **(ii)** if v_b(y_a) < v_b(y_b) and LPT_b separates y_a from y_b: b cuts by LPT_b and a chooses;
- **(iii)** if v_b(y_a) < v_b(y_b) and LPT_a separates y_a from y_b: a cuts by LPT_a and b chooses.

*Proof.* Each outcome is EFX: the chooser takes its favourite bundle, and the cutter's bundle is EFX-feasible by
Fact L.
- **(i)** Every good other than y_a is worth at most v_b(y_b) < v_b(y_a). So y_a and y_b are b's top two goods, and
  LPT_b puts y_a in P and y_b in Q.
  - If a takes P, the result is A1.
  - If a takes Q, then v_a(Q) > v_a(P) ≥ v_a(y_a), and v_b(X_b ∖ y_b) = v_b(P) ≥ v_b(y_a) > v_b(y_b). This is B.
- **(ii)** y_b is b's top good, so it lies in P, and y_a lies in Q.
  - If a takes Q, the result is A1.
  - If a takes P, then P ≠ {y_b}, since otherwise v_a(y_b) > v_a(Q) ≥ v_a(y_a), contradicting that y_a is a's top.
    By Fact L for b, v_b(Q) ≥ v_b(P ∖ min P) ≥ v_b(y_b), and genericity makes this strict, since Q ≠ {y_b}. Also
    v_a(P) > v_a(Q) ≥ v_a(y_a). This is B.
- **(iii)** y_a is a's top good, so it lies in P, and y_b lies in Q.
  - If b takes Q, the result is A1.
  - If b takes P, then P ≠ {y_a}, since otherwise v_b(y_a) > v_b(Q) ≥ v_b(y_b), contrary to the case. By Fact L for
    a, v_a(Q) ≥ v_a(P ∖ min P) ≥ v_a(y_a), strictly by genericity. Also v_b(X_b ∖ y_b) = v_b(P) > v_b(Q) ≥ v_b(y_b).
    This is B. ∎

**Coverage** (`kgen/n2_lpt.py`, `results/kgen_n3/n2_lpt_1_1500_3_11.log`; 3,000 random positive generic starts with
m = 3–11):
- (i)–(iii) cover 2,600 starts, and every outcome checks out as A1 or B.
- In the other 400, neither LPT separates the picks. An A1 or B allocation exists in all 400, and it is usually A1.
- **Fallback constructions for that case** (`kgen/n2_fallback.py`, `results/kgen_n3/n2_fallback_1_3000_3_11.log`):
  - an LPT followed by moving one pick across; an LPT without one pick that is then added; an LPT with both picks
    placed first;
  - together they cover 786 of the 802 open-case starts. Placing the picks first does best: 739.
  - 16 starts resist all of them, e.g. a = (619870, 991189, 107193, 945216, 332850, 32076) and
    b = (23407, 26682, 681099, 567713, 9653, 984770). There the picks are 1 and 5, and the A1 allocation
    ({0, 1, 2, 4}, {3, 5}) is not an LPT outcome.
- **The ratio knife** (`kgen/n2_knife.py`). b keeps y_b and a keeps y_a. b takes the junk in decreasing order of
  v_b/v_a and stops at the shortest prefix after which it does not strongly envy a's bundle. a keeps the rest.
  - On 6,000 starts it is EFX, and so of shape A1, on 5,920; with Lemma 2 (i)–(iii) on all 6,000.
  - It covers all 802 starts of the open case.
  - It fails only where LPT_b separates the picks, which is where Lemma 2 (i)–(ii) apply.
- **Conjecture K′ is refuted.** K′ said: if LPT_b puts y_a and y_b in the same bundle, the ratio knife gives an EFX
  allocation.
  - A generic counterexample with m = 6: a = (665099, 700695, 821454, 83536, 272439, 771165) and

  - It fails on 3 more of 13,144 generic starts in `results/kgen_n3/n2_kprime_5_40000_3_13.log`.
  - In every failure LPT_a separates the picks, so Lemma 2 (iii) applies there.
- **Conjecture K″ is refuted too.** K″ said: if neither LPT_a nor LPT_b separates the picks, the ratio knife gives an
  EFX allocation.
  - It held on the 5,572 + 802 open-case starts of the first tests, but fails on one generic instance with m = 7:
    a = (668666, 491967, 825539, 720622, 821267, 885493, 184361) and
    b = (939351, 90770, 928909, 995524, 818163, 853113, 466384), from both orders.
  - With order (0, 1), the picks are 5 for a and 3 for b. The knife gives a the bundle {1, 4, 5}, worth 2,198,727 to

  - Over 40,000 random instances (`kgen/n2_open.py 11 40000 3 12`, `results/kgen_n3/n2_open_11_40000_3_12.log`) the

- **A cleaner case split** (`kgen/n2_k3.py`, `results/kgen_n3/n2_k3_11_40000_3_13.log`). Suppose
  v_b(y_b) > v_b(y_a). Define two junk-mass conditions:
  - (Mb): the junk that b values above y_a is worth at least v_b(y_b) to b;
  - (Ma): the junk that a values above y_b is worth at least v_a(y_a) to a.

  If (Mb) fails, LPT_b separates the picks: y_b is never the poorer bundle's good before y_a arrives. If (Ma) fails,
  LPT_a separates them. Both implications are proved this way, and checked on 25,319 starts each. So Lemma 2 leaves
  exactly the starts where (Ma) and (Mb) both hold, which strictly contains the open case.
  - There the knife works on 30,590 of 30,592 generic starts and on all 9,644 starts with zeros
    (`n2_k3_12_15000_3_11_zeros.log`). The 2 failures are the instance above (Conjecture K‴, refuted with K″).
- **Status of the two-agent case.**
  - Proved for positive generic values, and for values generic on the relevant goods with zeros, outside the open
    case (Lemma 2, Lemma M, Fact L₀).
  - In the open case, about 13% of random starts, a construction exists on all data (the knife or F5/F6), but there
    is no proof. *(Proved in §9, case 2.)*
  - Even with two agents, no single cut-and-choose of all of M gives the OC₁ shapes at every k. *(§9's case 2 cuts
    M ∖ {y_a, y_b} instead.)*
- **A remark on the knife.** Along the ratio prefixes S_0 ⊂ S_1 ⊂ … (b holds {y_b} ∪ S_s), a's EFX condition
  can only switch from true to false, and b's only from false to true. For a it holds because
  v_a(B) − min_B v_a cannot decrease when a good is added to B; for b it holds by the same fact applied to a's
  shrinking bundle. So the knife finds A1 exactly when some ratio prefix gives an EFX allocation. On the instance above none does.
- **Open** *(all three closed by §9)*:
  - a proof for the open case;
  - ties;
  - agents without a pick. With zeros, cut-and-choose does not suffice
  for any cutter or feasible partition on 2 of 1,000 starts (`kgen/n2_cutchoose.py`), so the zero case needs a direct
  construction.

**With zero values** (values generic on each agent's relevant goods: no two different subsets of R_i have equal
value). Two additions make Lemma 2's cases go through.
- **Fact L₀.** In LPT_c, process c's positively valued goods as before, then its worthless goods, each to the poorer
  bundle. They all go to the same bundle, which is the poorer one, or the first one on a tie.
  - The bundle without worthless goods is EFX-feasible against the other by Fact L.
  - The bundle Z₀ holding the worthless goods is the poorer one, so the other bundle is worth at least v_c(Z₀). That
    is what EFX₀ asks when a good worth 0 is removed from Z₀.
- **Lemma M (two agents).** Let X be EFX₀ with the chooser c envying nobody, v_c(X_c) ≥ v_c(X_d). Moving any set W of
  c's worthless goods to d keeps X EFX₀.
  - c's value is unchanged, and v_c(X_d ∪ W ∖ g) ≤ v_c(X_d) ≤ v_c(X_c).
  - For d, and every g in the new X_c: v_d(X_c ∖ W ∖ g) ≤ v_d(X_c ∖ g) ≤ v_d(X_d) ≤ v_d(X_d ∪ W). ∎
- **The cases.**
  - In cases (i) and (ii), the chooser a moves all its worthless goods to b. a then holds only goods it values, so the
    one-absorber condition holds. The proofs of (i) and (ii) go through:
    - b's bundle only gains;
    - genericity on R_b gives the strict inequalities;
    - if v_b(y_a) = 0 or P has b-worthless goods, Fact L₀ gives v_b(Q) ≥ v_b(P).
  - In case (iii), the chooser b moves its worthless goods other than y_a to a. If b takes Q, the result is A2 (b holds
    only goods it values: a t = 0 move at b, with a absorbing). If b takes P, the result is B, with b's base containing
    y_a even when v_b(y_a) = 0.
- **Check** (`kgen/n2_zeros.py`, `results/kgen_n3/n2_zeros_1_3000_3_11.log`): 3,000 random instances with zeros and
  m = 3–11, 5,971 starts.
  - Cases (i)/(ii): 4,101 starts, cases (iii): 1,076, and the open case, handled by the ratio knife with a-worthless
    goods first, then Lemma M: 794.
  - Every outcome has an OC₁ shape.

So Lemma 2 holds with zeros, except the open case, which had no proof here. Ties and agents without a pick
(y_b missing: 29 starts skipped) were open too. §9 closes all three.

**Which steps use n = 2.**
- Cut-and-choose itself.
- The fact that the chooser is envy-free, so its shape conditions follow from its choice.
- Fact L, which concerns 2-partitions.

At n = 3 none of these is available as stated.

## 9. The two-agent theorem (PROVED, every k, any values)
**Setting.**
- Two agents a (the first picker) and b, any additive values v_a, v_b ≥ 0: ties and zeros allowed, k arbitrary.
- y_a is a's favourite good, and y_b is b's favourite in M ∖ {y_a}, among goods the agent values positively, ties
  to the smaller index (`sd` in `kgen/oc2.py`). If a values nothing, y_a does not exist; if b values nothing in
  M ∖ {y_a}, y_b does not exist.
- J = M ∖ {y_a, y_b} is the set of unpicked goods.
- min_c(Z) is c's smallest value in Z. Zeros count, so EFX₀ for c towards Z reads v_c(own) ≥ v_c(Z) − min_c(Z).

**Theorem 2.** From either order, the picks of two agents have an EFX₀ completion of OC₁(t ≥ 0) form, at every k.
It has one of these shapes:
- (A1) both keep their picks, and every good of X_a other than y_a is a good of J that a values. This is a t = 0
  move at a (H₀ = X_a ∖ y_a, when nonempty), with b the only agent holding goods outside its base.
- (B) y_a ∈ X_b, and X_a ⊆ J ∪ {y_b} consists of goods that a values, with v_a(X_a) > v_a(y_a) and v_b(y_a) > v_b(y_b).
  This is a chain a → b with t = 1: b's base becomes {y_a} ∪ (X_b ∩ J ∩ R_b), which b values above y_b, and a's base
  becomes H₀ = X_a. b is the only agent holding goods outside its base.
- (D) a degenerate case, in which an agent values nothing or b values only y_a (item 0 below).

In every shape, one agent holds no good outside its base. It is the output of a cut-and-choose computable in
O(m log m) (`kgen/theorem2.py`).

Three facts for arbitrary values are used.
- **Fact L (any values).** Let LPT_c place the goods in order of decreasing v_c (ties by index), each into the bundle
  of smaller current v_c (the first on a tie). Then the result (P, Q) is EFX₀ for c in both directions:
  v_c(Q) ≥ v_c(P) − min_c(P) and v_c(P) ≥ v_c(Q) − min_c(Q).
  - *Proof.* Let h be the last good placed in a nonempty bundle Z. The order is decreasing, so v_c(h) = min_c(Z).
    When h was placed, Z ∖ h was worth no more than the other bundle, which only grew afterwards. So
    v_c(Z) − min_c(Z) ≤ v_c(other). Goods worth 0 need no separate treatment. ∎
  - If the first good in LPT_c's order has positive value, the first two goods go to different bundles: the first
    goes to P, and the second to the then empty Q.
- **Lemma M (any values; §8).** Let (X_c, X_d) be EFX₀ with v_c(X_c) ≥ v_c(X_d). Moving any set W of goods of X_c that c
  values at 0 into X_d keeps the allocation EFX₀ and keeps v_c(X_c) ≥ v_c(X_d).
  - c's value is unchanged, and v_c((X_d ∪ W) ∖ g) ≤ v_c(X_d) ≤ v_c(X_c).
  - For d and every g in X_c ∖ W, v_d((X_c ∖ W) ∖ g) ≤ v_d(X_c ∖ g) ≤ v_d(X_d) ≤ v_d(X_d ∪ W). ∎
- **The Tops Lemma (any n).** Suppose every agent i has a good t_i that it values at least as much as every good, with
  the t_i distinct. Let S be an EFX₀ allocation of M ∖ {t_i}. Then X_i = S_i ∪ {t_i} is EFX₀.
  - *Proof.* Fix i ≠ j. Removing t_j from X_j leaves S_j. If S_j = ∅, there is nothing to check. Otherwise
    v_i(S_j) = (v_i(S_j) − min_i(S_j)) + min_i(S_j) ≤ v_i(S_i) + v_i(t_i), because S is EFX₀ and t_i is i's top.
  - Removing g ∈ S_j leaves v_i(S_j ∖ g) + v_i(t_j) ≤ v_i(S_i) + v_i(t_i), by EFX₀ in S and v_i(t_j) ≤ v_i(t_i). ∎
  - Only "t_i is i's top" is used, against t_j and against one good of S_j. So the lemma holds as long as each agent
    values its own t_i at least as much as every other t_j and every good of M ∖ {t_i}.

*Proof of Theorem 2.* Fix the order (a, b).
0. **Degenerate cases (D).**
   - If a values nothing, X = (∅, M). b keeps y_b and absorbs.
   - If b values nothing, X = (M, ∅). a keeps y_a and absorbs.
   - If b values only y_a, there is no y_b.
     - If v_a(y_a) ≥ v_a(M ∖ y_a), take X = ({y_a}, M ∖ y_a): a keeps its pick and is EFX₀, and b absorbs.
     - Otherwise take X = (M ∖ y_a, {y_a}). This is a chain a → b with t = 1: b gains v_b(y_a) > 0, and a's base
       becomes the goods of M ∖ y_a that a values, worth more than v_a(y_a). b values a's bundle at 0, and b's
       bundle is a singleton, so the allocation is EFX₀. a is the only agent with goods outside its base.

   From here on, y_a and y_b exist, and v_a(y_a), v_b(y_b) > 0.
1. **v_b(y_a) > v_b(y_b).** Then y_a is b's unique top good, and y_b comes next in b's order (ties by index, as
   in `sd`).
   - b cuts M by LPT_b: y_a ∈ P, y_b ∈ Q (Fact L). a takes P if v_a(P) ≥ v_a(Q), and Q otherwise. Then a's goods
     worth 0 to a move to b (Lemma M). The result is EFX₀, by Fact L for b, a's choice, and Lemma M.
   - If a took P: y_a ∈ X_a (v_a(y_a) > 0), y_b ∈ X_b, and X_a ∖ y_a ⊆ P ∖ y_a ⊆ J consists of goods a values.
     This is shape A1.
   - If a took Q, then v_a(X_a) = v_a(Q) > v_a(P) ≥ v_a(y_a), and X_a ⊆ Q ⊆ J ∪ {y_b} consists of goods a values.
     y_a ∈ P ⊆ X_b, and v_b(y_a) > v_b(y_b). This is shape B. (y_b may have moved to b, if a values it at 0; it is
     then outside b's new base, and b is still the only agent with goods outside its base.)
2. **v_b(y_a) ≤ v_b(y_b).** Then y_b is b's top good in all of M, and y_a is a's top good.
   - b cuts J by LPT_b into (P, Q). a takes its better part S_a (P on a tie), b gets S_b, and a's goods worth 0 to a
     move from S_a to S_b (Lemma M on J).
   - The result is an EFX₀ allocation of J, by Fact L, a's choice and Lemma M.
   - By the Tops Lemma with t_a = y_a and t_b = y_b, X = (S_a ∪ {y_a}, S_b ∪ {y_b}) is EFX₀.
   - Both keep their picks, and X_a ∖ y_a = S_a ⊆ J consists of goods a values. This is shape A1. ∎

**Literature.**
- Bare EFX₀ existence for two agents is known. Plaut–Roughgarden (arXiv 1707.04769v3, SODA 2018) prove it in
  Theorem 4.3: "For two players with general (not necessarily identical) valuations, Algorithm 2 returns an EFX
  allocation." The proof is cut-and-choose with the leximin++ solution.
- Their Definition 2.3 quantifies over every g ∈ A_j, so their EFX is EFX₀. "General" means normalized and monotone.
- For two additive agents, the closing paragraph of their Appendix B gives a polynomial-time method: player 1 cuts by
  Algorithm 4 with two copies of herself, and player 2 chooses.
- Read via the arXiv HTML rendering of v3, through a fetch subagent that returned verbatim quotes; the PDF was not
  read. The referee's note `k4/referee/lit_n2.md` on `claude/k4-referee` points to the same theorem.
- **What is new here** is only the shape: picks kept with one t = 0 move, or one chain a → b, so that each agent keeps
  its distinct favourite when it has one. Also new are the Tops Lemma and the O(m log m) construction. The referee
  found no published source for the shape.

  - A first version of item 0 handed y_a to b when b values nothing. b does not gain from that, so it is not a chain.
    A random run of 20,000 instances caught it on 122 starts, all sparse or zero-heavy; that log was not kept. The

### Lemma T (open; Theorem 2 no longer depends on it)
**Lemma T (conjecture).** Let two agents have distinct top goods t_a ≠ t_b. Among the allocations X with t_a ∈ X_a,
t_b ∈ X_b and b not strongly envying a, every one maximizing v_a(X_a) is EFX₀.
- It implies **Claim T**: two agents with distinct favourite goods have an EFX₀ allocation in which each keeps its
  favourite good. Claim T is now proved directly, by case 2 of Theorem 2's proof.
- **Evidence.** Lemma T and Claim T hold on all 6,094 instances with distinct tops in `kgen/n2_claimT.py 7 9000 11`
  (`results/kgen_n3/n2_claimT_7_9000_11.log`): generic values, small integers with ties, and zeros. They also hold on
  the 2,001 instances of `kgen/n2_lemmaT.py` (`n2_lemmaT_1_3000_2_10.log`).
- **Toward a proof by local improvement** (`n2_lemmaT.py`). Consider every state in which b does not strongly envy a
  but a strongly envies b: 15,392 generic, 16,980 small-integer and 4,939 zero-value states.
  - Each has an improvement of one of these types, which keeps b satisfied and raises v_a(X_a):
    - add: one good of b's bundle moves to a;
    - swap: one good each way;
    - bulk-1: the non-top parts are exchanged, except one of b's goods.
  - Add or swap alone suffices on all but one generic state. That state needs a two-for-one exchange: from
    ({1, 6, 7}, {0, 2, 3, 4, 5}) to ({0, 1, 4, 7}, {2, 3, 5, 6}).
  - So a proof must allow multi-good exchanges, or argue directly about a global maximizer.
- **A partial argument.** Let X = (A, B) be a maximizer and suppose a strongly envies B.
  1. Since t_a ∈ A is a's top, B has at least three goods.
  2. The bulk exchange Z = ({t_a} ∪ (B ∖ t_b), {t_b} ∪ (A ∖ t_a)) raises v_a. So, by maximality, b strongly envies a
     in Z.
  3. With X feasible, this gives v_b(B ∖ t_b) > v_b(A ∖ t_a) + (v_b(t_b) − v_b(t_a)) + min_b({t_a} ∪ B ∖ t_b): b is
     rich in its own non-top goods.
  4. Moving b's b-cheapest non-top good h to a keeps b satisfied as soon as
     2·v_b(h) ≤ v_b(t_b) − v_b(t_a) + v_b(B ∖ t_b) − v_b(A ∖ t_a). That raises v_a, a contradiction, unless that
     inequality fails.
  5. What is missing is the case where it fails: v_b(t_a) close to v_b(t_b) and b's non-top goods of similar value.
     There, swaps and two-for-one exchanges are needed.
- **Cut-and-choose does not prove it.** In 8 of 8,000 starts neither agent has a cut separating the two tops that is
  EFX₀-feasible for it both ways (`kgen/n2_sepcut.py`). Example: a = (826800, 873560, 30285, 758210),
  b = (467097, 106311, 880483, 772384). Claim T's allocation ({0, 1}, {2, 3}) is EFX₀ only for its actual owners.

**Lemma T reduced to one canonical exchange** (`kgen/n2_canon.py`; logs `n2_canon_2_2500_9.log` with m ≤ 9,
`n2_canon_3_600_12.log` with m ≤ 12 and `n2_canon_4_160_14.log` with m ≤ 14).
- **States.** Let X = (A, B) with t_a ∈ A and t_b ∈ B, in which b does not strongly envy a but a strongly envies b.
  Write A₀ = A ∖ t_a, B₀ = B ∖ t_b and ρ(g) = v_a(g)/v_b(g), with ρ = ∞ when v_b(g) = 0; ties go by index.
- **Three exchanges:**
  - **C1:** add to A the good g* of B₀ with the largest ρ among the goods a values;
  - **C2:** swap g* with the good x* of A₀ with the smallest ρ;
  - **C3:** a takes {t_a} ∪ (B₀ ∖ h) and b takes {t_b, h} ∪ A₀, where h is a's cheapest good in B₀.
- **Conjecture E.** In every such state, one of C1, C2, C3 keeps b unenvious and raises v_a(A). Then no maximizer of
  Lemma T can be such a state, which proves Lemma T.
- **Evidence.** It holds on every state:
  - with m ≤ 9: 7,801 generic, 7,799 small-integer and 2,844 zero-value states;
  - with m ≤ 12: 14,657 generic, 9,522 small-integer and 3,870 zero-value states;
  - with m ≤ 14 (`n2_canon_4_160_14.log`): 13,577 generic, 12,213 small-integer and 196 zero-value states.

  That is 72,479 states in all.
- **Which pairs suffice.**
  - "C1 or C3" covers everything with m ≤ 9, but misses 6 generic states with m ≤ 12.
  - "C1 or C2" misses 49 states with m ≤ 12 (37 of them generic), and "C2 or C3" misses many.
  - So all three are needed.
- **What is proved of Conjecture E** (written proofs):
  1. *C1 raises v_a* (g* is valued by a), and *C3 raises v_a* in every such state.
     - Proof for C3: a strongly envies B, so v_a(B) − min_a(B) > v_a(A).
     - If t_b is a's cheapest good in B: v_a(A₃) = v_a(t_a) + (v_a(B) − v_a(t_b)) − v_a(h) > v_a(A) + v_a(t_a) − v_a(h)
       ≥ v_a(A).
     - Otherwise h is a's cheapest good in B: v_a(A₃) = v_a(t_a) − v_a(t_b) + (v_a(B) − v_a(h)) > v_a(A).
     - Both steps use that t_a is a's top. ∎
  2. *If A = {t_a}, C1 works.* b keeps t_b, its top, so v_b(B ∖ g*) ≥ v_b(t_b) ≥ max(v_b(t_a), v_b(g*)). ∎
  3. *Adding a good g to A keeps b unenvious iff s ≥ v_b(g) + max(v_b(g), μ).* Here μ = min_b(A) and
     s = v_b(B) − v_b(A) + μ ≥ 0 is b's slack. This is a direct computation from b's EFX₀ condition.
  4. *C3 keeps b unenvious iff s − μ ≤ 2(v_b(t_b) − v_b(t_a)) + 2v_b(h) + min_b(A₃).* By item 3, if C1 and C3 both
     fail, then v_b(g*) > (v_b(t_b) − v_b(t_a)) + v_b(h) + (min_b(A₃) + μ)/2. So b values g* above h.
- **Missing.** The implication "C1 and C2 fail ⟹ C3 works". It must use that g* has the largest ratio in B₀ and x* the
  smallest in A₀, since the inequalities of items 3–4 alone are consistent with C3 failing.

**Algorithm.** Theorem 2's construction is one sort and one LPT pass, O(m log m), with no case left open.
- Lemma T's maximizer is a knapsack-like problem with no polynomial bound proved. It is not needed.
- The ratio knife and F5/F6 of §8 are superseded.

**Where the two-agent proof uses n = 2** (assignment Step 3).
- **Cut-and-choose**, in cases 1 and 2. It needs a single cutter whose cut is EFX₀ for it both ways (Fact L, a
  statement about 2-partitions) and a single chooser, who is then envy-free.
- **Lemma M** needs the chooser to be envy-free, which cut-and-choose gives for free. With three agents, the
  worthless goods of one agent must go to someone who stays satisfied.
- **The shape count.** With two agents, everyone keeping their pick and receiving extra goods is OC₁: a t = 0 move at
  a and b absorbing. With three agents, one t = 0 move and one absorber leave the third agent at most one good
  outside its base. Keeping all three picks can then be impossible, even with distinct tops (§10, the tops example).
- **Not n-specific.** The Tops Lemma, which is stated and proved for every n; that the first picker's pick is its top
  good; and the case split on v_b(y_a) against v_b(y_b), which at n agents becomes "who prefers which earlier pick
  to its own".

## 10. n = 3 at k = 4, 5: where the two-agent argument breaks
**The Tops construction at n = 3: keeping every pick fails** (`kgen/tops3.py`, `results/kgen_n3/tops3.log`).
- Instance: three agents, v_i(g) = 10 for every good except v_i(i) = 11. So goods 0, 1, 2 are distinct tops, and
  they are the picks of every order.
- The Tops Lemma (§9) makes every EFX₀ allocation of the rest EFX₀ with the tops added. But OC₁ allows one t = 0 move
  and one absorber, so the third agent may get at most one good besides its pick.
- *Proof that this fails for m ≥ 9.* Let z be that agent, worth at most 21 to itself. Every other bundle, holding a
  pick and p goods, is worth at least 10(p + 1) to z. Minus one good, that is at least 10p, so EFX₀ for z needs
  p ≤ 2. So at most 3 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 8 goods are placed.
- *Check.* For m = 9 and 10, `oc3.c`'s shape enumeration finds EFX₀ completions only for t = 1 and t = 2 chains, in
  all six orders, and none for "picks" or "t = 0". An independent Python count, over all 3^m allocations, finds no EFX₀
  allocation that keeps all picks and leaves some agent at most one extra good. For m = 6, 7, 8 such allocations exist
  (6, 36 and 90 of them).
- So the n = 3 analogue of §9's case 2 must move the picks, by a chain of length t ≥ 1 such as a rotation
  x₀ → x₁ → x₂. In this instance, a rotation that gives every agent one extra good in its base, plus one further good,
  is EFX₀ for m = 9. Cut-and-choose plus "hand the tops back" does not lift as it stands.

**Distinct tops at n = 3: which shapes are needed** (`kgen/dtops3.py`; logs `results/kgen_n3/dtops3_{1..4}_1500_6_11.log`).
- Instances: the six kinds of `gen3.py` with m = 6–11, after which good i becomes agent i's unique top. So the picks
  are (0, 1, 2) in every order.
- Shape families, from `oc3.c`:

  - *t1/z*: a chain x₀ → x₁ with the third agent z absorbing;
  - *t1/x*: a chain with x₀ or x₁ absorbing;
  - *t2*: a chain through all three agents.

- **Pivot P1.** z gets its top plus at most one good, and the other two split the rest by Theorem 2's case 2. It

- **The hand-off shape is loose.** With positive generic values, it asks only for an EFX₀ allocation in which z holds

  An illustration only, from a scratch enumeration (not kept) of six hard "ident" instances with m = 9: z typically

**Proposition H (calibration: n = 3 at every k is as hard as EFX for three agents).** Suppose that every
three-agent instance with distinct tops, and relevance bound k + 3, has an EFX₀ allocation. Then every three-agent
instance with relevance bound k has an EFX₀ allocation. In particular, OC₁ at n = 3 for every k, even restricted to
distinct tops, implies EFX₀ for three additive agents. That is the Chaudhury–Garg–Mehlhorn theorem (JACM 2024; PROMPT.md
§2, not read here), in its EFX₀ form via L1.
- *Proof.*
  - Given an instance I on M, add goods t₁, t₂, t₃. Agent i values t_i at L + δ and t_j (j ≠ i) at L, where
    L > max_i v_i(M) and δ > 0. Then t_i is i's unique top, and the relevance bound grows by 3.
  - Let X be EFX₀ for the new instance. Suppose agent i holds no t. Then some agent j holds two. For every g ∈ X_j,
    X_j ∖ g still contains a t, so v_i(X_j ∖ g) ≥ L > v_i(M) ≥ v_i(X_i), contradicting EFX₀. So each agent holds
    exactly one t, say t_{σ(i)}, and X_i = {t_{σ(i)}} ∪ S_i with S an allocation of M.
  - For g ∈ S_j, EFX₀ gives v_i(S_i) ≥ v_i(S_j ∖ g) − (v_i(t_{σ(j)}) − v_i(t_{σ(i)})) ≥ v_i(S_j ∖ g) − δ. So S is
    EFX₀ for I up to an additive δ.
  - Take δ → 0. Some S among finitely many repeats infinitely often, and the inequalities are closed, so it is EFX₀
    for I. ∎
- So a proof of OC₁ at n = 3 for every k, or of Conjecture D3, would be a new proof of three-agent EFX. Theorem 2 is
  the n = 2 case, where existence is easy: Plaut–Roughgarden, Theorem 4.3 (§9, Literature).
- The reduction maps relevance bound k to k + 3. So it says nothing about OC₁ at n = 3 for a fixed small k, e.g.
  k ≤ 4, which is the campaign's case. A bounded-k statement at n = 3 remains a reasonable target.

**The lift of Lemma T tested** (`kgen/lemmaT3.py`; reachability by Lemma R, ported from `oc3.c` and equal to it on all
360 starts checked).

- Let F be the set of complete allocations that are OC₁(t ≥ 0)-reachable from the picks and in which every agent other
  than z is EFX₀-satisfied.
- Maximize v_z(X_z) over F, for z the first picker (as in Lemma T) or the last.

**What breaks.**
- With two agents, the single other agent's EFX₀ constraint is a down-set in a's bundle (§8 remark). So any good can

- With three agents, the two other agents' constraints also depend on each other's bundles. A maximizer for z can

- **Maximum-matching starts.** The k = 4 break above is on a maximum-matching start: all three agents get picks. So it
  stands under the coordinator's OC₁^max restriction, where only orders whose picks form a maximum matching count.

**Conjecture T₃.** For three agents, every k, every order and z the first or the last picker, some maximizer of
v_z(X_z) over F is EFX₀. It holds on all 2,880 starts tested for each role.
- If proved together with OC₁-reachability of its witness (built into F), it implies OC₁ for three agents.
- It is no weaker than OC₁, but it locates a potential argument: a maximizes, and the others' EFX₀ conditions are the
  constraints. A proof would need a rule for choosing among maximizers.

## 11. What Theorem 2 gives for n ≥ 3 (proved)
**Corollary 3 (every n, every k).** Fix an order π. Suppose n − 2 of the agents, z₁, …, z_{n−2}, have picks that are
*iteratively top-heavy*: v_{z_r}(y_{z_r}) ≥ v_{z_r}(M ∖ {y_{z₁}, …, y_{z_r}}) for each r. Then OC₁(t ≥ 0) holds for π,
with z₁, …, z_{n−2} keeping their picks as singletons.
- *Proof.* Induction on r with Lemma P (§2).
  - Lemma P at step r is applied to the instance without z₁, …, z_{r−1} and their picks. There the hypothesis reads
    exactly as above, and the other agents' picks are unchanged (Lemma P, item 1).
  - After n − 2 steps two agents remain, and Theorem 2 gives OC₁(t ≥ 0) for every two-agent instance and order.
  - Each peeled agent adds a singleton bundle and no good outside its base, so the absorber count stays at most one. ∎
- At n = 3 this reads: **OC₁(t ≥ 0) holds for every order in which some agent's pick is worth at least as much to it
  as all other goods together**, at every k.
- This is PROMPT.md's peeling rule R1, carried over to OC₁ form, with the two-agent base case now proved.
- Not covered at n = 3: orders in which no agent's pick is top-heavy. By Proposition H (§10), the general case is
  as hard as three-agent EFX.

## 12. The simpler three-agent EFX proof, read against OC₁ (coordinator's route (b))
**Source.** H. Akrami, N. Alon, B. R. Chaudhury, J. Garg, K. Mehlhorn, R. Mehta, "EFX Allocations: Simplifications
and Improvements", arXiv 2205.07638v2 (AACGMM below).
- Read in its arXiv LaTeX source: the sections Preliminaries, Technical Overview, and "EFX Existence beyond
  Additivity" (its main theorem, label `mainthm1`), and the Appendix (non-degeneracy).
- Not read: its rainbow-cycle sections, CGM's original proof, and whether the EC version under the title
  "EFX: A Simpler Approach and an (Almost) Optimal Guarantee via Rainbow Cycle Number" is the same paper [unverified].

**What it proves.** Three agents, with v₁ and v₂ monotone and v₃ "MMS-feasible", have an EFX allocation (`mainthm1`).
- MMS-feasible means: for every S and every two 2-partitions (A₁, A₂), (B₁, B₂) of S,
  max(v(B₁), v(B₂)) ≥ min(v(A₁), v(A₂)).
- It contains the nice-cancelable valuations (their Lemma `general-nice`), and so the additive ones.
- Strong envy is defined over every g ∈ X_{i′}, so "EFX" there is EFX₀ here.

**Q2: zero values.** They are handled, so the theorem is EFX₀.
- The proof assumes non-degenerate instances: no agent values two different sets equally, "implying that all goods
  are positively valued by all agents".
- The Appendix reduces the general case to this one, for monotone valuations: v′_i(S) = v_i(S) + ε·Σ_{g_j ∈ S} 2^j
  preserves every strict preference, so an EFX allocation of the perturbed instance is EFX, over all goods, for the
  original.
- This is L1 of PROMPT.md in general form. Proposition H (§10) is consistent with it: existence with distinct tops
  carries no new content at n = 3, and what OC₁ adds is the shape.

**The algorithm** (`kgen/aacgmm3.py` implements it for additive values).
- It keeps a *partition* (X₁, X₂, X₃), not yet assigned to agents, with two invariants: X₁ and X₂ are EFX-feasible
  for agent 1, and X₃ is EFX-feasible for agent 2 or agent 3.
- The potential is φ = min(v₁(X₁), v₁(X₂)).
- Start: agent 1 runs Plaut–Roughgarden (PR) with three copies of itself, so every bundle is EFX-feasible for it,
  and agent 2 takes its favourite bundle as X₃.
- Step: if some assignment of the bundles makes every agent EFX-feasible, stop. Otherwise X₃ is "too valuable" to
  agents 2 and 3.
  - Case 1: some i ∈ {2, 3} still prefers X₃ ∖ g_i to X₁ ∪ g_i. Move g_i into X₁, shrink X₁ ∪ g_i to an
    inclusion-minimal subset that agent 1 values above X₁, and return the rest to X₃. Re-run PR(v₁) if needed.
  - Case 2: otherwise, agent 2 cuts X₁ ∪ X₃ into two by PR, agent 3 chooses, and agent 1 takes X₂. If agent 1
    strongly envies a bundle, it is compressed, or PR(v₁) is re-run.
- φ strictly increases, so the algorithm terminates.

**Q1: can it run from an SD start as a sequence of chains, rings or rounds?** Not as written.
- It moves through partitions, and agents meet bundles only at the end, by choice. Nothing tracks an individual good:
  PR moves and compressions carry picks between bundles freely. So its output has no built-in relation to the picks.
- Termination comes only from φ increasing over finitely many partitions. The section read states no bound on the
  number of steps.
  - In the implementation, the outer loop ran up to 128 times at m ≤ 10.
  - In zero-heavy instances the counts cluster at powers of two (2, 4, 8–9, 16–17, 32–33, 64–65, 128). This is
    consistent with φ climbing through the 2^j perturbation one worthless good at a time. That is evidence, not a
    bound.
- **Experiment** (`kgen/aacgmm3.py`, logs `results/kgen_n3/aacgmm3_{1..4}_1200_5_10.log`; 4,800 instances with
  m = 5–10, generic, near-identical and zero-heavy, made non-degenerate exactly as in the Appendix). For each order,
  the output was tested for OC₁(t ≥ 0)-reachability from the picks, by Lemma R:

| kind | starts | OC₁-reachable, agent 1 of AACGMM = first picker | for some assignment of the three roles | all picks kept |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| general | 9,600 | 6,833 | 9,463 | 3,362 |
| near-identical | 9,600 | 8,195 | 9,557 | 803 |
| zero-heavy | 9,600 | 6,315 | 9,464 | 5,658 |

  - So the AACGMM allocation is an OC₁ completion only 66–85% of the time, depending on the kind (6,315 / 9,600 to
    8,195 / 9,600). On 316 of 28,800 starts no role assignment makes it one.
  - Example: values (88, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0), (0, 0, 0, 0, 93, 0), (13, 0, 2, 0, 46, 0), order (0, 1, 2), picks 0, 4, 2.
    The completion ({0}, {4}, {1, 2, 3, 5}) is OC₁. Every AACGMM output moves good 0 or good 4 to agent 2, or gives
    agent 0 the junk.
- **What an OC₁ variant would need.** An invariant that ties picks to bundles before bundles meet agents.
  - With distinct tops, the natural attempt is to run AACGMM on M ∖ {tops} and hand the tops back (Tops Lemma). It
    gives an EFX₀ allocation with every top at home. §10's tops example shows that this placement can be incompatible
    with OC₁, so a variant must route the tops along a chain.
  - AACGMM's cut-and-choose structure (agent 1 cuts, agents 2 and 3 choose) does not do that.
  - I see no direct way to phrase its steps as §29 chains (my reading).

**Q3: where it uses n = 3** (my reading of the section).
1. *The invariant and the finish.* Agent 1 owns two EFX-feasible bundles out of three, and the third is feasible for
   agent 2 or 3. The finish is a hand-checked matching of three bundles to three agents (the paper's footnote lists
   the cases).
   - At n = 4 one needs a Hall-type invariant: which sets of agents find which bundles feasible.
2. *Case 2 is a two-agent cut-and-choose.* Agent 2 cuts X₁ ∪ X₃ into two by PR, and agent 3 chooses. Agent 3's
   MMS-feasibility, a statement about 2-partitions, guarantees that its chosen bundle beats X₂, the bundle agent 1
   then takes.
   - At n = 4, the analogue needs three agents to split the union of the other bundles EFX₀-wise, with a guarantee
     relative to agent 1's bundle. That is a three-agent statement with a lower bound, which neither AACGMM nor CGM
     provides as far as I read.
3. *The potential* is one agent's minimum over two bundles. At n = 4, agent 1 would hold three, and several agents
   could all find only the same bundles feasible.
- PROMPT.md §2 lists additive EFX as open in general; I did not check the four-agent literature beyond that.

**Conclusion for the campaign.**
- At n = 3, existence is AACGMM's theorem (EFX₀, zeros included).
- OC₁ asks for more: the EFX₀ allocation must arise from the picks through one chain. AACGMM's allocation does not
  satisfy that in general, and its potential argument works on unassigned partitions.
- A proof of OC₁ at n = 3 via AACGMM needs a new invariant that carries the picks, not just a re-reading.

**Which move breaks, and why** (smallest instances: `kgen/aacgmm3_opb_min.py`,
`results/kgen_n3/aacgmm3_opb_min_1_6000.log`. Values are small integers v, used as v·2^{m+1} + 2^j. In every one, an
OC₁ completion with one pick per bundle exists).
- **F1: Plaut–Roughgarden's start is impossible under OPB.** AACGMM starts from a partition in which *all three*
  bundles are EFX-feasible for agent 1. With one pick per bundle, such a partition need not exist.
  - Proof by example: goods 0–3, values (2, 0, 0, 1), (0, 2, 0, 1), (0, 0, 2, 1), order (0, 1, 2), picks 0, 1, 2.
  - Good 3 must join some pick. If it joins agent 0's pick, agent 0 strongly envies {0, 3} from a bundle worth 0 to
    it. If it joins another pick, the bundle minus that pick is worth 1 > 0.
  - Checked: none of the 18 OPB partitions is EFX for agent 0, raw or perturbed.
  - Yet 24 arrangements satisfy AACGMM's weaker invariants, and ({0}, {1}, {2, 3}) is an EFX₀ OC₁ completion.
  - So the start, not the invariant, is what fails. Constrained PR halts at a bundle whose cheapest good for agent 1
    is its pick, and which still beats the poorest bundle without that pick. The smallest instance found:
    m = 4, (1, 2, 1, 2), (0, 0, 0, 0), (1, 1, 0, 2), order (1, 0, 2).
- **F3: the compression is no longer inclusion-minimal.** AACGMM's Observation (`X1X2EFXfeasible`) needs X₁′
  inclusion-minimal among sets beating X₁: then agent 1 values X₂ above every X₁′ ∖ h.
  - With the pick pinned, X₁′ ∖ (pick) can still beat X₁. So X₂ can fail to be EFX-feasible for agent 1 because of
    X₁′. The PR repair then starts from a minimum below φ, and the potential falls.
  - Smallest instance: m = 5, (7, 4, 8, 0, 0), (5, 7, 6, 0, 6), (0, 5, 8, 4, 0), order (0, 1, 2), roles (1, 0, 2).
- **F4: g_i must avoid the pick.** AACGMM's Observation `noEFXfeasibility` and its case 2 use that X₃ ∖ g_i is i's
  best proper subset of X₃.
  - When i's cheapest good of X₃ is X₃'s pick, the restricted g_i breaks this. The case 2 exit (agent 1: X₂;
    2 and 3: the two-agent cut-and-choose) can then leave agent 2 or 3 envying X₂.
  - Smallest instance: m = 6, (0, 9, 0, 2, 7, 8), (3, 7, 0, 0, 9, 8), (2, 2, 5, 4, 8, 3), order (0, 1, 2), roles (1, 0, 2).
- **Ending EFX₀ but not OC₁.**
  - Either the matching is a permutation whose receiver does not gain (smallest: m = 3, (1, 0, 1), (1, 1, 2),
    (0, 0, 0)), or it is the identity with every bundle holding three or more goods.
  - Preferring an OC₁ matching at the finish removes most of these: the identity cases drop from 4,138 to 2,684
    (general) and from 2,580 to 30 (near-identical). It does not touch F1–F5.

**Conclusion.**
- AACGMM with one pick per bundle fails in three places:
  1. Plaut–Roughgarden (F1, F2), because single-good moves cannot respect pinned picks, and its "all three bundles
     feasible" start can be impossible;
  2. the compression Observation (F3), because a pinned pick makes the compressed set non-minimal;
  3. the choice of g_i (F4).
- On the same data, the invariant itself, one pick per bundle with AACGMM's two-plus-one feasibility, is always
  satisfiable, and an OPB OC₁ completion always exists.
- A candidate repair would replace PR by a rebalancing step that keeps one pick per bundle and targets only agent 1's
  two bundles, and would compress "minimal among sets containing the pick", with a potential that tolerates it.
  Neither is designed or tested yet.
- **Conjecture OPB₃** (2,880 of 2,880 random starts, m ≤ 8): at n = 3, if every agent has a pick, then from every
  order some EFX₀ OC₁ completion has exactly one pick in each bundle. **Refuted in §14.2** on k = 4 cores, at m = 5.

## 14. The repair: AACGMM with one pick per bundle, version 2 (computational, n = 3)
**Changes** (`kgen/aacgmm3_opb2.py`), aimed at §13's failures.
- **REB replaces PR.** It rebalances only agent 1's two bundles X₁ and X₂, with X₃ a dump. While X₁ or X₂ is not
  EFX-feasible for agent 1, let X_l be the poorer of the two and (j, g) a violation, v₁(X_j ∖ g) > v₁(X_l).
  - If some violating g is not a pick, move g to X_l.
  - Otherwise only X_j's pick violates. Then exchange the non-pick parts of X_j and X_l. This is allowed when X_j is
    the dump, or when X_j's pick is worth more to agent 1 than X_l's.
  - Each move raises φ = min(v₁(X₁), v₁(X₂)) strictly.
- **Repairs start from φ(X′).** After the pinned compression of case 1 or case 2, the repair is REB starting from
  φ(X′) > φ(X), not PR from a three-bundle minimum. So F3 of §13 is gone.
- **The start and "both envied".** The constrained three-bundle PR, stopping where it stops, then agent 2 chooses,
  then REB. In "both envied" the three-bundle minimum starts at v₁(X₂) > φ and never falls.
- **Invariant 2** (X₃ feasible for agent 2 or 3) is checked after every REB.
  - Option RECHOOSE: when it is lost, agent 2 re-takes its favourite bundle, and REB runs again. φ can then fall.
  - Option DUMPSWAP: a blocked REB swaps the richer bundle's pick with the dump's pick, when the dump's pick is worth
    more to agent 1 than some non-pick good of the richer bundle. φ is unchanged, and a strict move follows.
- **The finish** prefers a feasible matching that is an OC₁ completion.
- **Termination.** Without RECHOOSE, φ rises strictly in every step, so the procedure always terminates: it ends in
  an EFX₀ allocation or in one of the failure types below.

### 14.1 One pick per bundle and the one-move / two-move boundary
**Observation (any n).** Let X be EFX₀ with exactly one pick in each bundle, and let σ be the permutation in which
agent i holds y_{σ(i)}.
- Each non-trivial cycle i₁ → i₂ → … of σ is a ring, the chain of Lemma R in which x₀ takes x_t's pick.
- Suppose every agent on a non-trivial cycle *gains*: v_i(y_{σ(i)}) + v_i(X_i ∩ J ∩ R_i) > v_i(y_i). Then X is a
  completion of the bases after c disjoint rings, c being the number of non-trivial cycles. Ring agents hold only base
  goods and goods worth 0 to them.
- Since c ≤ ⌊n/2⌋:
  - At n = 3, c ≤ 1. So an OPB EFX₀ allocation with improving rings is an OC₁ completion, given the absorber
    condition: positive values, and when c = 0 some agent holds at most one good besides its pick.
  - At n = 4, two disjoint swaps (cycle type 2+2) can be forced.
- So, if OPB holds, n = 3 is one move for a structural reason: three picks admit only one non-trivial cycle.

**Check at n = 4** (`kgen/opb_n4.py`, `results/kgen_n3/opb_n4.log`). Each instance is run at its failing order and
enumerated over all 4^m allocations, with raw EFX₀ over every good. "Gains" means v_i(X_i) > v_i(y_i) for every agent
with σ(i) ≠ i. With one pick per bundle, this is exactly Lemma R's ring gain.
- **The two counterexamples of `attempts/kgen-n4-oc1.md`** (k ≥ 5):
  - Instance A (m = 8, order (0, 3, 1, 2)): 127 EFX₀ allocations. 63 have one pick per bundle, and all 63 have
    cycle type 2+2 with every moved agent gaining.
  - Instance B (m = 7, order (0, 2, 1, 3)): 33 EFX₀ allocations, of which 12 have one pick per bundle. Exactly one,
    ({0, 2, 5}, {6}, {3, 4}, {1}), has every moved agent gaining, and it is of type 2+2.
  - So these two are repaired by two disjoint rings.
- **The k = 4 instance behind the campaign's OC₁^max refutation** (sent by the coordinator, 2026-11-26). Sets
  [[0,1,2,5],[1,2,5,6],[3,4,5,6],[3,4,6]], values [[2,10,4,7],[10,3,4,8],[3,5,6,7],[3,2,4]], m = 7, order (0, 3, 2, 1),
  picks (1, 2, 5, 6).
  - 14 EFX₀ allocations, the count the coordinator and other sessions found.
  - 4 have one pick per bundle: two of type 2+1+1, one of type 3+1 and one of type 4. **In none does every moved
    agent gain.** Agent 3, which gives up good 6 (worth 4 to it), never recovers 4.
  - **So Conjecture OPB₄ is false, already at k = 4.** The same holds from order (1, 3, 2, 0)
    (`results/kgen_n3/opb4_known.log`).
- *Correction.* An earlier version of this subsection said OPB₄ "fits OC₂ at n = 4" from instances A and B alone,
  and the coordinator's numbers contradicted it. Both are right: they concern different instances. The code and its
  EFX₀ and gain tests reproduce the coordinator's 14 / 4 / 0 exactly on the k = 4 instance.
- **Test of OPB₄ on random instances** (`kgen/opb4.py`, logs `opb4_{1..4}_150_6_9.log`; 600 instances, m = 6–9).
  OPB₄ holds on all 3,523 distinct pick vectors: the fewest rings needed is 0 on 3,455 and 1 on 68. Random instances
  miss both kinds of n = 4 failure.

**What survives of the framing.**
- At n = 3, OPB₃ would have explained one move, since three picks admit only one non-trivial cycle. It is false (§14.2).
- At n = 4 there are two kinds of OC₁ failure. Instances A and B are repaired by two disjoint rings, as one-pick-
  per-bundle permutations of type 2+2. The k = 4 instance has no one-pick-per-bundle repair at all. There, the
  coordinator reports that one round of disjoint chains fails and a second round is needed. The two rounds can move
  a pick twice, which a single permutation of picks does not describe.
- So "one pick per bundle" explains part of the n = 4 picture, not all of it.

### 14.2 OPB₃ is false (exhaustive check on the k = 4 cores, n = 3)
**Check** (`kgen/opb3_cover.py`, log `results/kgen_n3/opb3_cover.log`). Every strict balanced type profile of the 51
n = 3 cores of `results/k4_certs_3.json.gz`, and every order.
- Types are this script's own enumeration: 6 for 3 goods and 288 for 4, plus the core condition C4 for two private
  goods. For every core they equal the list's `domains`.
- For every maximum matching Y with three picks, every one-pick-per-bundle allocation gives a box of profiles in which
  it is EFX₀ and every moved agent gains, and, for "OPB₃", satisfies the absorber condition.
- Every SD region (per order) must lie inside the union of the boxes. This is the coverage method of
  `k4/oc1cert/brute.py` (branch `compute/k4-oc1max-cert`), reimplemented here.
- Spot check: the box answer equals a direct brute force from integer values on random profiles (at the end of the
  log).

**Result.** Many cores have uncovered profiles, for OPB₃ and even for "OPB with gains only" (no absorber condition).
So OPB₃ is false at n = 3 and k = 4.

**Smallest counterexample** (m = 5, a core).
- Relevant sets [[0, 1, 2, 4], [1, 3, 4], [2, 3, 4]], values [[3, 6, 10, 2], [4, 2, 3], [4, 2, 3]] (strict, balanced),
  order (1, 2, 0). Agent 1 picks 1, agent 2 picks 2, and agent 0 picks 0.
- There are 5 EFX₀ allocations, listed by an independent brute force:
  - ({0, 1}, {3, 4}, {2}), ({0, 2}, {1}, {3, 4}), ({2}, {0, 1, 3}, {4}) and ({2}, {0, 1}, {3, 4}) each put two picks in
    one bundle;
  - ({2}, {1, 3}, {0, 4}) has one pick per bundle, but agent 2, which gave up good 2 (worth 4), gets 3.
- OC₁ holds there (`oc3.c`: 3 witnesses, shapes t = 1 from 1 → 0 and from 2 → 0). An example is ({0, 1}, {3, 4}, {2}):
  - agent 0 receives agent 1's pick, worth 6 > 3, and keeps its own pick 0 as its one good outside the base;
  - agent 1 takes {3, 4} from the junk, worth 5 > 4;
  - agent 2 keeps its pick.
- So the OC₁ repair needs the *receiver* to keep its own pick as an extra good. That puts two picks in one bundle,
  which no permutation of the picks describes.

**Consequence.**
- "One pick per bundle" fails at both n = 3 (this instance) and n = 4 (§14.1).
- At n = 3 the one-move repairs are chains x₀ → x₁ in which x₁ may keep y_{x₁} as its single good outside its base.
  The pick placement is then not a permutation. One chain hands x₀'s pick to x₁, and x₀ is compensated from the junk.
- The random instances of §13.2 (generic, near-identical and zero-heavy kinds) never hit this. The strict balanced
  cores do, already at m = 5.
- §13–§14's constrained AACGMM was built on OPB, so its coverage figures describe a heuristic, not an invariant that
  could be proved.

**15.2 Lemmas (proved).** Throughout: a perfect draft after LB's upgrades, valid with (UT) (`proofs/lb_last_step.md` §3).
- **Lemma G1.** If T = {t}, Theorem B\* applies iff |E_t| = 1.
  - B\*'s (i) and (ii) hold for every k ∈ E_t. k is not t, not upgraded (it holds its top), so it is frozen. Every
    frozen agent's need chain ends at a terminal, and the only terminal is t.
  - (iv) asks for a hitting set of size |T| − 1 = 0 for the sets {b_x, c_x} ∖ {b_k, c_k} (x ∈ E_t ∖ {k}), so each
    {b_x, c_x} lies inside {b_k, c_k}, i.e. equals it. That contradicts (iii) unless E_t = {k}.
  - Conversely, if E_t = {k}, then h(E_t) = 1 = |T|, and (iii) and (iv) are vacuous. ∎
  - So the one-terminal gap is exactly: T = {t}, |E_t| ≥ 2 (and |J| ≥ 2, since |J| ≤ |T| completes).
- **Lemma G2.** If T = {t} and E_t ≠ ∅, then t holds b_t or c_t.
  - If t held a_t, it would need nothing. A need chain's last link is a good t needs, so no need chain ends at t.
    Since t is the only terminal, there would be no frozen agent.
  - Then every agent other than t is upgraded, so E_t = ∅. ∎
- **Lemma G3 (after a rotation into t).** Let k ∈ E_t and k = x₀ → … → x_s = t be a need chain. Rotate as in B\*:
  each x_i (i ≥ 1) takes Y_{x_{i−1}}, k takes {b_k, c_k} ⊆ W = J ∪ {Y_t}, and Y_t is released. Then:
  - P′ is valid (B\* (a)–(c), which use only k ∈ E_t, the need chain, and that t is a terminal).
  - **E′_k = E_t ∖ {k}**, and π′_x = {b_x, c_x} ∖ {b_k, c_k} for these x.
    - The inclusion "⊆" is B\* (e). t is excluded because {b_t, c_t} ⊄ W. If Y_t = c_t, then b_t ∈ N_t was picked by
      another agent (I1). If Y_t = b_t, (UT) gives c_t ∉ J.
    - "⊇": an exposed x ≠ k holds its top, so it needs nothing and is not a middle agent of the chain. It keeps a_x,
      and {b_x, c_x} ⊆ W = J′ ∪ {b_k, c_k}.
  - **So owner k works after the rotation iff** no x ∈ E_t ∖ {k} has {b_x, c_x} = {b_k, c_k}, and the sets
    {b_x, c_x} ∖ {b_k, c_k} (x ∈ E_t ∖ {k}) have a hitting set of at most |T′| goods (Lemma 1 (b)). Otherwise no
    owner is needed if |J′| = |J| − 1 ≤ |T′|.
  - Everything therefore turns on |T′|: how many agents the rotation *releases*.

**15.3 Candidate Theorem T1, and its evidence** (`kgen/k3max_t1.py`, `results/kgen_n3/k3max_t1_*.log`).

> **T1.** At a perfect draft with T = {t} after LB's upgrades, |E_t| ≥ 2 and |J| ≥ 2: among the rotations from some
> k ∈ E_t along a need chain into t, take one that gives t its most preferred good. Its result has a one-absorber
> completion (lb2, with LB's upgrades).

| data | gap drafts | some rotation into t repairs | every best-for-t rotation repairs | every rotation repairs | no owner needed after the first repairing rotation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| n = 3 | 72 | 72 | 72 | 72 | 72 |
| n = 4 | 1,040 | 1,040 | 1,040 | 984 | 1,024 |
| n = 5, m = 9 | 1,744 | 1,744 | 1,744 | 1,704 | 1,340 |

**Structure of the gap** (`kgen/k3max_t1stats.py`, `results/kgen_n3/k3max_t1stats_*.log`; n = 5, m = 9, 1,744 drafts).
- U = ∅ at the draft in every case.
- t holds c_t in 1,680 cases and b_t in 64.
- |J| = 4, with |E_t| = 2 (1,328) or 3 (416).
- The exposed pairs are pairwise distinct in 1,720 and share a common good in 976.
- t never wants every exposed top. So the repair is not just "t gets its top and releases everyone".
- The best rotation gives t its top in 1,680 cases (its b in 64), with chain length 1–3. Afterwards t is again a
  terminal in 1,632 cases, and an owner is needed in 400.

**15.4 T1 in its "best for t" form is false; the counting form T1′ survives.**
- On the n = 5, m ≤ 8 cores (`k3max_t1_small_5_*.log`: 23,768 gap drafts, all with |T| = 1), some rotation into t
  repairs every one. But the best-for-t rotation fails on 24 of them.
- Example: rankings 0: 5 ≻ 0 ≻ 6, 1: 5 ≻ 3 ≻ 7, 2: 0 ≻ 4 ≻ 3, 3: 4 ≻ 1 ≻ 2, 4: 5 ≻ 1 ≻ 2; m = 8; picks
  (0, 7, 3, 4, 5); J = {1, 2, 6}; T = {1}; E_t = {3, 4}.
  - The exposed agents 3 and 4 have the *same* pair {1, 2}.
  - The best-for-t rotation 4 → 1 gives agent 1 its top 5. But agent 0 still wants 5, and agent 3's pair then lies
    inside the new pair holder's bundle, so owner 4 is excluded. Only agent 2 is released, against J′ = {6, 7}.
  - The rotation 3 → 2 → 1 releases agents 1 and 2, and J′ = {6, 7} goes to them, with no owner.
- **Every least-ω′ rotation into t repairs** (ω′ = |J′| − |T′|, before or after LB's upgrades). This holds on all
  26,624 gap drafts with n ≤ 5, matching `k4/k3_oc1.md`'s OT-ω for DE trades. In every case the least ω′ is ≤ 0.

> **T1′ (counting form).** At a perfect draft whose state after LB's upgrades has T = {t} and |E_t| ≥ 2, some rotation
> from some k ∈ E_t along a need chain into t, followed by LB's upgrades, gives |J′| ≤ |T′|. Its completion then needs
> no owner (Lemma 1 (a)), and every bundle has at most two goods.

- **Evidence** (`kgen/k3max_t1all.py`, `results/kgen_n3/k3max_t1all_*.log`). T1′ holds on every such draft, hard or
  not:
  - n = 3: 96;
  - n = 4: 1,184;
  - n = 5, m = 9: 1,928;
  - n = 5, m ≤ 8: 27,248.

  That is 30,456 in all, none failing. Lemma 2's identity ω′ = (m − 2n) + e′ + ν′ is asserted after every rotation.
- **Dead goods** (`kgen/k3max_t1rel.py`, `results/kgen_n3/k3max_t1rel_small_4_dead{0,1}.log`). With one dead good
  added to the n = 4 cores, the least ω′ is positive on 16 of 1,184 such drafts, so the no-owner form fails there.
  - T1′ is therefore stated for the **ledger's cores**, where every good is valued by someone. PROMPT.md's L3 has
    already removed goods valued by nobody.
  - In DE's general core case with dead goods, the target must allow an owner (Lemma G3's conditions).
- **Where the releases come from** (n = 4, least-ω′ rotation). The rotation releases one or two agents besides t:
  - one exposed agent and one chain agent in 776 of 1,184 cases;
  - otherwise one exposed agent, one chain agent, or another agent.

  LB's upgrades after the rotation contribute in 372 cases, and t is again a terminal in 948.
- **Why it is a counting statement.**
  - The rotation always gives |J′| = |J| − 1: k takes two goods of W = J ∪ {Y_t}, and Y_t is released unless k takes
    it.
  - An upgrade moves one junk good to an agent leaving T, so it leaves |J| − |T| unchanged.
  - By Lemma 2, with e = 0 throughout, ω′ ≤ 0 iff ν′ ≤ 2n − m = (n − 1 − |U|) − (|J| − 1).
  - So T1′ says: some rotation into t, with the releases its upgrades cause, makes at least |J| − 1 previously
    wanted goods unwanted.

**15.5 Choosing the rotation: cycles** (`kgen/k3max_t1acyc.py`, `results/kgen_n3/k3max_t1acyc_*.log`).
- A rotation into t can create a cycle in the want graph and leave no terminal at all. At n = 5, m ≤ 8, for |J| = 2
  and |E_t| = 2, some rotation does so on 1,072 of 14,352 drafts. So the rotation must be chosen.
- **Lemma G4 (proved).** If every receiver x_i (1 ≤ i ≤ s) of the rotation receives its own top, the want graph stays
  acyclic. So some non-pair-holder is a terminal.
  - *Proof.* After the rotation the receivers hold their tops and want nothing, so every want arc into a receiver is
    gone. A cycle would therefore avoid the receivers and k (now a pair holder).
  - The holdings and wants of the other agents are unchanged, so such a cycle existed before the rotation. But the
    draft's want graph is acyclic: it is a need order of serial dictatorship. ∎
- So for |J| = 2 a "top chain" into t completes, if one exists (|J′| = 1 ≤ |T′|). But t gets its top in some
  rotation on only 22,264 of the 23,768 gap drafts at n = 5, m ≤ 8, and the other receivers need not get theirs.
  The general choice must be by releases, i.e. least ω′.

**15.6 Rule E, and T1′ for |J| ≤ 2 (proved)** (`kgen/k3max_ruleE.py`, `results/kgen_n3/k3max_ruleE_*.log`).

*Rule E.* Fix a topological order σ of the draft's need DAG (holders before wanters). From k ∈ E_t, the next
receiver is always the σ-earliest wanter of the current good, until t is reached. Every frozen agent has a wanter,
and t is the only terminal, so the chain ends at t.

- **Lemma G5 (proved).** The rotation along a Rule E chain keeps the want graph acyclic, also after LB's upgrades.
  - *Proof.* We show that every want arc after the rotation, u → w ("w wants the good u now holds"), goes forward
    in σ.
  - *Into a receiver x_i* (1 ≤ i ≤ s). x_i now wants only goods above Y_{x_{i−1}}, which it wanted before as well.
    - If such a good g did not move, its holder precedes x_i in σ (holders before wanters).
    - If g moved, g = Y_{x_{j−1}} is now held by x_j.
      - j = i is impossible: that is the good x_i now holds.
      - j > i is impossible too. x_i ranks g above Y_{x_i}, so x_{j−1} precedes x_i in the need DAG. But the chain gives
        x_i < x_{i+1} < … < x_{j−1}, which would be a cycle.
      - So j < i, and x_j precedes x_i, since the chain increases in σ.
  - *Out of a receiver x_i.* The wanters of Y_{x_{i−1}} other than x_i are later than x_i, because Rule E chose x_i
    as the σ-earliest of them. A chain agent x_j that wants Y_{x_{i−1}} after the rotation wanted it before, since
    its wants only shrink. So this holds for it too.
  - *Other agents.* Non-chain agents keep their goods and wants. A non-chain u wanted by a receiver x_i precedes it,
    since x_i wanted Y_u before.
  - *k.* It is now a pair holder: it wants nothing, and nobody wants b_k or c_k (validity, B\* (c)). So it has no arcs.
  - LB's upgrades only remove arcs: an upgraded agent wants nothing, and its b and c are not wanted. ∎
  - Lemma G4 is the special case in which every receiver gets its top.
  - Checked by the k3-oc1 session (2026-11-27), which found G5 and G5′ correct. The case split above for j is their
    suggested expansion.
  - Rule E is used only for the arcs out of receivers. An arbitrary need chain can create a cycle (§15.5).
- **Corollary G5′ (T1′ for |J| ≤ 2; proved).** If |J| ≤ 2, the Rule E rotation from any k ∈ E_t gives
  |J′| = |J| − 1 ≤ 1 ≤ |T′|.
  - t is not a pair holder, so some agent is outside U′. An acyclic want graph on those agents has a sink, and a
    sink holds a good nobody wants: a terminal.
  - Upgrades never raise |J| − |T|.
  - So the draft has a one-absorber completion after one DE trade, with every bundle at most two goods.
- **Coverage.** |J| = 2 is 720 of the 1,184 one-terminal drafts with |E_t| ≥ 2 at n = 4, and 14,928 of 27,248 at
  n = 5, m ≤ 8. |J| = 1 is not hard.
- **Data for the rest.** Rule E keeps the want graph acyclic for every k on all 30,456 drafts with n ≤ 5. Some k
  with a Rule E chain reaches |J′| ≤ |T′| on all of them. So T1′ holds with Rule E as the canonical choice of chain;
  only the choice of k remains.

- **Choosing k for |J| ≥ 3** (same script and logs). With Rule E chains, no simple canonical k works:

  | data, \|J\| | drafts | σ-first k fails | σ-last k fails | "top that t ranks highest" fails | some k works |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | n = 4, \|J\| = 3 | 320 | 16 | 36 | 52 | 320 |
  | n = 5, m = 9, \|J\| = 3 | 184 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 184 |
  | n = 5, m = 9, \|J\| = 4 | 1,744 | 352 | 492 | 804 | 1,744 |

  So for |J| ≥ 3 the choice of k has to use the releases themselves, e.g. least ω′ over the Rule E rotations.

**15.7 n = 6, and the right target T1-E** (`kgen/k3max_gapfile.py` on k3-oc1's complete gap lists
`results/k4_k3oc1/gap_*.gap.gz`, branch `claude/k4-k3-oc1` @ 974853c; logs `results/kgen_n3/k3max_gapfile_*.log`).
- **Scope.** At n = 6, m ∈ {10, 11} (every core, profile and order) the one-terminal gap has 96,128 drafts, with
  |J| = 3, 4 or 5. k3-oc1 also finds 10,304 two-terminal gap drafts there, which are their part.
- **Lemma G5 holds** on all 96,128, as the proof says it must.
- **T1′ (no owner) is false at n = 6.** On 360 one-terminal gap drafts, no rotation into t from E_t reaches
  |J′| ≤ |T′|, over all need chains.
  - Example: rankings [[1,0,7],[0,2,8],[1,3,9],[0,5,4],[6,2,4],[3,6,5]], m = 10, order (0,1,2,4,5,3), picks
    (1,0,3,4,6,5).
  - So the counting form, and k3-oc1's n ≤ 5 observation that least ω always reaches the no-owner case, stop at n = 5.
- **With an owner allowed, a stronger and canonical form holds everywhere:**

> **T1-E.** At a perfect draft of a ledger core (k = 3) whose state after LB's upgrades has one terminal t and
> |E_t| ≥ 2, for **every** k ∈ E_t, the rotation along k's Rule E chain into t has a one-absorber completion
> (lb2: LB's upgrades, then no owner, a terminal owner or a pair-holder owner).

| data | one-terminal gap drafts | T1-E (every k) | T1′ (no owner, some rotation) |
|---|---|---|---|
| n = 4 | 1,040 | 1,040 | 1,040 |
| n = 5, m = 9 | 1,744 | 1,744 | 1,744 |
| n = 5, m ≤ 8 | 23,768 | 23,768 | 23,768 |
| n = 6, m ∈ {10, 11} | 96,128 | 96,128 | 95,768 |

T1-E needs no choice at all: Rule E fixes the chain, and any exposed k works. Corollary G5′ proves it for |J| ≤ 2,
with no owner. For |J| ≥ 3 an owner is needed in general. k3-oc1's Lemma 4 is the natural tool (`k4/k3_oc1.md` §12 on
`claude/k4-k3-oc1` @ 974853c, read there):
- **Lemma 4 (k3-oc1).** "If a DE trade gives k its pair {b_k, c_k}, then afterwards no agent x with
  {b_x, c_x} ∩ {b_k, c_k} ≠ ∅ is exposed for any owner o ≠ k."
- *Proof (theirs).* "Exposure needs {b_x, c_x} ⊆ J′ ∪ base(o), but a good of {b_x, c_x} lies in k's bundle, which is
  neither junk nor o's base. ∎"

**15.8 How the Rule E rotation completes** (`kgen/k3max_owner.py`, `results/kgen_n3/k3max_owner_*.log`; every
k ∈ E_t of every one-terminal gap draft at n = 4 and n = 5, m = 9, and of the first 8,000 such drafts at n = 6).
- **The owner lb2 reports is the first it finds**, trying no owner, then the terminals, then the pair holders. So the
  counts below describe that search order, not which owners are possible.
  - In this sample, lb2 never needed owner k.
  - *Correction:* an earlier version said "owner k is never used". k3-oc1 shows that on the 360 n = 6 drafts where
    no owner fails, owner k completes ([[1],[2,8,7],[3],[0,4],[6],[5,9]] in their example).
- **Most rotations complete with no owner**, after 0–3 of LB's upgrades:
  - n = 4: all 2,080 rotations;
  - n = 5, m = 9: 3,692 of 3,904;
  - n = 6 sample: 18,144 of 19,328.
- **The rest use owner t** (100 at n = 5; 872 in the n = 6 sample), **another pair holder** (112; 304), or **another
  terminal** (8 at n = 6).
- **The simple test** (LB's maximal upgrades, then Lemma 1 (a), (b) or (c) of `k4/k3_oc1.md` §2) passes on all
  of them except 64 n = 6 rotations. Those need lb2's extra freedom: a subset of upgrades, or a pair holder's slot.
- So a proof of T1-E for |J| ≥ 3 must count the releases from the upgrades that the rotation enables (chain agents
  now holding their b with their c in the junk), and must allow owner t.

**15.9 The target, in B\*'s form: T1-k** (`kgen/k3max_owner.py`, `results/kgen_n3/k3max_owner_*.log`).

> **T1-k.** At a perfect draft of a ledger core (k = 3) whose state after LB's upgrades has one terminal t and
> |E_t| ≥ 2, some k ∈ E_t has this property. Rotate k along its Rule E chain into t, apply LB's maximal upgrades,
> and then the simple test of Lemma 1 (`k4/k3_oc1.md` §2) passes with no owner or with owner k. That is, either
> |J′| ≤ |T′|, or no x ∈ E′_k has {b_x, c_x} = {b_k, c_k} and h(E′_k) ≤ |T′|.

- By Lemma G3, E′_k = E_t ∖ {k} with π′_x = {b_x, c_x} ∖ {b_k, c_k}, computed in the rotated state, before
  upgrades. So T1-k is B\*'s count with |T′|, the terminals after the rotation and its upgrades, in place of |T| − 1.
  What it adds over B\* is the credit for releases.
- The form was suggested by k3-oc1 (`k4/k3_oc1.md` §12 on `claude/k4-k3-oc1`): "some rotation into a terminal, then
  Lemma 1 with owner none or k".

| data | one-terminal gap drafts | T1-k (some k, Rule E chain) | rotations failing "owner none or k" for that k |
|---|---|---|---|
| n = 4 | 1,040 | 1,040 | 0 of 2,080 |
| n = 5, m = 9 | 1,744 | 1,744 | 112 of 3,904 |
| n = 5, m ≤ 8 | 23,768 | 23,768 | 72 of 49,040 |
| n = 6, m ∈ {10, 11} | 96,128 | 96,128 | 2,472 of 210,816 |

- So T1-k holds on all 122,680 one-terminal gap drafts with n ≤ 6.
- "Every k" fails in this owner form, so the k must be chosen, unlike T1-E (§15.7), which uses any owner.
- With R1C, B\*, and k3-oc1's two-terminal extension of B\* (their part), T1-k would complete OC₁ at
  maximum-matching starts at k = 3, for the ledger's cores, at every n.

**15.10 T-E: every k, owner none, t or k, with k's slot** (k3-oc1's form, `k4/k3_oc1.md` §12 and
`k4/k3oc1/t2k_e.py` on `claude/k4-k3-oc1`; checked here independently by `kgen/k3max_te.py`,
`results/kgen_n3/k3max_te_*.log`).

> **T-E (one terminal).** At a perfect draft of a ledger core (k = 3) whose state after LB's upgrades has one terminal
> t and |E_t| ≥ 2, take **any** k ∈ E_t. Rotate k along its Rule E chain into t and apply LB's maximal upgrades. Then
> a completion exists with owner o ∈ {none, t, k}, in which:
> - every terminal other than o takes at most one junk good;
> - k, if k ≠ o, takes at most one junk good g, such that {b_k, c_k, g} contains no other top-holder's pair ("k's slot").
>
> If t was upgraded, owner t means t as a pair holder.

- **Soundness of k's slot.** In a valid DE state, the only agents that can strongly envy a bundle {b_k, c_k, g} with
  g junk are top-holders x with {b_x, c_x} inside it.
  - A b-holder or c-holder wants only goods that are held alone elsewhere.
  - A pair holder values any two goods at most a < b + c.
  - The slot condition excludes the top-holders. So this is Theorem 1′ (`proofs/lb_last_step.md` §2) with one more
    bundle of three goods.
- **The independent check.**
  - The completion test is written separately from `lbtest.lb2`. It matches a hit part of the junk into the slots,
    the owner takes the rest, and the bundles are checked against Theorem 1′'s threats.
  - Only `parts`, `upgrade`, `exposed` and the Rule E chain are shared code.

| data | drafts | rotations (every k) | all complete | no owner | owner t | owner k |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| n = 4 | 1,040 | 2,080 | yes | 2,080 | 0 | 0 |
| n = 5, m = 9 | 1,744 | 3,904 | yes | 3,692 | 212 | 0 |
| n = 5, m ≤ 8 | 23,768 | 49,040 | yes | 48,968 | 72 | 0 |
| n = 6, m ∈ {10, 11} | 96,128 | 210,816 | yes | 202,156 | 8,532 | 128 |

(Owners are tried in the order none, t, k.)

- The 128 rotations that need owner k agree with k3-oc1's count.
  - Owner t fails there because t now holds a_k, which exposes new agents for t.
  - Owner k sees only E′_k = E_t ∖ {k} (Lemma G3).
- **T-E is the one-terminal target.** It contains T1-k (§15.9) and needs no choice of k.
- It is proved for |J| ≤ 2 (Corollary G5′, no owner). The proof for |J| ≥ 3 must count releases, including those
  caused by upgrades, and use owners t and k.

**Status.**
- T1′ is proved for |J| ≤ 2 (Corollary G5′), and is false in general (n = 6).
- The target for the one-terminal gap is **T-E** (§15.10: every k, Rule E chain, owner none, t or k, with k's
  slot; k3-oc1's form, checked independently). It holds on all 122,680 such drafts with n ≤ 6 (366,840 rotations), and
  it is proved for |J| ≤ 2. T1-k (§15.9) and T1-E (§15.7) are weaker forms of it. T1 as first stated
  ("every best-for-t rotation") is refuted.
- A proof of OC₁^max at k = 3 needs:
  1. T1′;
  2. "every hard perfect draft with |T| ≥ 2 is covered by R1C or B\*", true on these data but unproved;
  3. R1C and B\* (proved, refereed).
- Split (agreed): the two-terminal gap to the k3-oc1 session (part 2 is false as stated at n = 6, so it becomes
  "B\* extended"), and the one-terminal part (T1-E) here.
