\title{\efxz{} Allocations with at Most Three Relevant Goods: A Short Proof by Exchange Cycles}
\subtitle{Long version}
\titlerunning{\efxz{} with at Most Three Relevant Goods: A Short Proof (long version)}

\author{Evan Lin}
\authorrunning{E. Lin}
\institute{University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
An allocation of indivisible goods is \efxz{} if no agent envies another agent's bundle after removing any single good from it, even a good that the envious agent values at zero. We give a short proof that every instance with nonnegative additive valuations in which each agent positively values at most three goods has a complete \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles but at most one have at most two goods, and that one can be computed in polynomial time by an algorithm we call Draft and Exchange (\DE{}). \DE{} lets agents that can leave with one good do so, drafts one good each for the others, and then, while no agent can absorb the leftover goods, exchanges goods along a cycle or a path of agents, each of which gets strictly better off; there are at most $4n$ exchanges. The heart of the proof is an \emph{Improvement Lemma}: a valid state from which no free agent can absorb the leftovers is Pareto-dominated by another valid state, obtained by one such exchange. Existence, and the shape for agents that value exactly three goods and are balanced, were machine-checked in Lean in previous work, by a much longer construction. The new proof, derived independently twice and refereed once with no error found (by an independent AI agent; there has been no human peer review), is machine-checked in Lean over the natural numbers, together with the algorithm and its bound of $4n$ exchanges; it was also checked by computer on millions of states.
\keywords{Fair division \and Indivisible goods \and EFX \and Serial dictatorship \and Exchange cycles}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{EFX and \efxz{}}\label{sec:intro-efx}

We allocate a set $M$ of $m$ indivisible goods to a set $N$ of $n\ge1$ agents. Agent $i$ has an \emph{additive valuation}: a value $v_i(g)\ge0$ for each good $g$, and $v_i(S)=\sum_{g\in S}v_i(g)$ for every set $S$ of goods. An \emph{allocation} $X$ assigns every good to exactly one agent; agent $i$'s \emph{bundle} $X_i$ is the set of goods assigned to $i$. So the bundles are pairwise disjoint and cover $M$: every allocation in this paper is \emph{complete}, and no good may be thrown away.

Envy-freeness can be impossible with indivisible goods (one good, two agents who value it). \emph{Envy-freeness up to any good} weakens it~\cite{AmanatidisBFV22}: the envy of $i$ for $X_j$ must disappear as soon as any single good is removed from $X_j$. Two versions differ in which goods may be removed:
\begin{align*}
\text{EFX:}&\quad v_i(X_i)\ge v_i(X_j\setminus\{h\})\quad\text{for all } i\ne j \text{ and } h\in X_j \text{ with } v_i(h)>0;\\
\text{\efxz{}:}&\quad v_i(X_i)\ge v_i(X_j\setminus\{h\})\quad\text{for all } i\ne j \text{ and } h\in X_j.
\end{align*}
Both conditions are vacuous when $X_j$ is empty. \efxz{} implies EFX, and the two coincide when all values are positive. Whether every instance with additive valuations has a complete EFX allocation is a central open question of fair division~\cite{AmanatidisBFV22}. The difference between the two notions is one rule: \emph{under \efxz{}, if another agent's bundle contains a good that agent $i$ values at $0$, then $i$ must not envy that bundle at all}, because removing that good does not lower its value to $i$.

\begin{example}[EFX, but not \efxz{}]\label{ex:efx-vs-efx0}
Two agents share three goods $a$, $b$, $c$. Agent~1 values $a$ at $3$, $b$ at $2$ and $c$ at $0$; agent~2 values only $c$, at $1$. Consider $X_1=\{b\}$ and $X_2=\{a,c\}$. Under EFX, agent~1 may remove only $a$ from $X_2$, since $c$ is worthless to it; what remains, $\{c\}$, is worth $0\le2$. Agent~2 holds $c$ and faces the single good $b$, which it values at $0$. So $X$ is EFX. It is not \efxz{}: removing $c$ from $X_2$ leaves $\{a\}$, worth $3>2$ to agent~1. The allocation $X_1=\{a\}$, $X_2=\{b,c\}$ is \efxz{}.
\end{example}

\subsection{Results}\label{sec:results}

Write $R_i=\{g\in M\st v_i(g)>0\}$ for the goods agent $i$ values, its \emph{relevant} goods. For $k\le2$, if every agent values at most $k$ goods, serial dictatorship works: the agents, in any order, each take a favourite remaining good, and the last agent takes everything left; the result is \efxz{} (\cite{MrdEfx}; Corollary~\ref{cor:k2} below). At three goods this breaks. An agent that values $a$, $b$, $c$ at $4$, $3$, $2$ may take $a$ and then see $b$ and $c$ end up in the last agent's bundle together with a third good; removing that third good, which it values at zero, leaves $b$ and $c$, worth $5>4$.

\begin{theorem}[existence]\label{thm:target}
Every instance with $n\ge1$ agents and nonnegative additive valuations in which every agent positively values at most three goods has a complete \efxz{} allocation.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[one large bundle]\label{thm:D}
Every such instance has a complete \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles but at most one have at most two goods. In particular this holds when every agent values exactly three goods and is \emph{balanced}, that is, values none of them more than the other two together.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[algorithm]\label{thm:algo}
Algorithm Draft and Exchange (\DE{}, Section~\ref{sec:algo}) computes, for every instance in which every agent positively values at most three goods, an allocation as in Theorem~\ref{thm:D}. It performs at most $n$ peeling steps and at most $4n$ exchanges, and each step takes time polynomial in $n$ and $m$. On real values we count, as in~\cite{LinK3}, a comparison of two sums of at most two values of one agent as one step; every decision of \DE{} is such a comparison.
\end{theorem}

\noindent \DE{} has three stages: \emph{peeling} (agents that can leave with one good do so), a \emph{draft} (the remaining agents take at most one good each), and \emph{exchanges} (goods move along cycles or paths of agents, each of which gets strictly better off), after which one agent, the \emph{absorber}, takes the leftover goods. Section~\ref{sec:overview} explains them.

\subsection{The new proof, and its status}\label{sec:status}

Theorems~\ref{thm:target} and~\ref{thm:D} were proved in previous work~\cite{LinK3} by a construction called \textsc{LB}$^+$ (algorithm K3ALG), whose proof uses blocks of a draft, leaders, an owner test, and one rotation of picks along a chain; the proof of its correctness is long. This paper replaces that proof by a short one. Its core is the Improvement Lemma (Theorem~\ref{thm:improve}): in a \emph{valid} state (Section~\ref{sec:states}) from which no free agent can absorb the leftovers, a single exchange along a cycle or a path gives a valid state in which every agent is at least as well off and some agent is strictly better off. Since utilities only grow, exchanges stop; when they stop, an absorber exists, and the completion is \efxz{} by a soundness theorem that needs nothing but validity (Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}).

\paragraph{Status.} We are explicit about what has been verified, and how.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Previous work.} Theorem~\ref{thm:target}, and Theorem~\ref{thm:D} for agents that value exactly three goods and are balanced, were machine-checked in Lean~4 in previous work~\cite{LinK3,Repo}, for values in any linearly ordered cancellative commutative monoid (which covers the nonnegative reals by a standard fact); the algorithm K3ALG of that work was machine-checked over the natural numbers, and over ordered values in a comparison model. That proof is different from the one here.
\item \emph{This paper.} The Improvement Lemma, the soundness theorem in the form used here, the algorithm \DE{}, its correctness, and Theorem~\ref{thm:D} for all instances with at most three relevant goods per agent are written proofs, and they are \emph{machine-checked in Lean~4} over the natural numbers (Section~\ref{sec:evidence}); the running time and real values are not formalized. The Improvement Lemma was derived independently twice, by two different arguments (Section~\ref{sec:second}). Its proof was refereed once, with no error found, by an independent AI agent (a separate session of the coding assistant that helped write this work, which did not write the proof); there has been no human peer review. We apply the referee's four presentation fixes. Every step of it has been checked by computer as described in Section~\ref{sec:evidence}: three independent implementations, two of this proof and one of the second, assert every claim on millions of states.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:status} in Section~\ref{sec:evidence} lists every claim with its evidence. Section~\ref{sec:overview} explains the approach informally; Sections~\ref{sec:prelim}--\ref{sec:algo} give the definitions, the proofs and the algorithm; Section~\ref{sec:limits} shows that the shape of Theorem~\ref{thm:D} cannot be improved in the obvious ways. The notes from which this paper is written, the test programs and the referee report are in the repository~\cite{RepoS}.

\subsection{Related work}\label{sec:related}

For additive valuations, complete EFX allocations are known to exist only in special cases, among them: for three agents~\cite{ChaudhuryGM20} and, more generally, for three types of agents (at most three distinct valuations)~\cite{HVGNV25}; when $m\le n+3$~\cite{Mahara21}; for four agents and at most nine goods~\cite{AlkassarFM26}; when every good is valued by at most two agents (multigraphs of agents, with goods as edges)~\cite{AfshinmehrAMMS26}, with special cases in~\cite{ChristodoulouFKS23,SgouritsaS25,KavianiSS24}; and for hypergraphs of girth at least four~\cite{LianeasSS26}. All of these use the all-goods form \efxz{}. For monotone valuations that are not additive, EFX allocations need not exist~\cite{AkramiMMSW26,MackenzieS26}. Table~\ref{tab:rw} compares these results with ours.

We restrict instead how many goods each agent values. For at most two goods per agent, serial dictatorship suffices and has been formalized in Lean~\cite{MrdEfx}. For at most four, Viswanathan and Mehta~\cite{ViswanathanM24} announce the existence of ordinary EFX allocations, with a polynomial-time algorithm and a proof sketch in an extended abstract. Their first phase, which gives a first choice, by a maximum matching, to as many as possible of the agents that value their top good more than their second and third together, resembles our peeling rule R1. Their algorithm ends by giving the goods that nobody values to an arbitrary agent, which is harmless for EFX but can break \efxz{}, as Example~\ref{ex:efx-vs-efx0} illustrates. Since \efxz{} implies EFX, Theorem~\ref{thm:target} proves the case of three goods of their result in full.

An instance with at most three relevant goods per agent and no complete \efxz{} allocation would refute the additive EFX conjecture (perturb all values by a small $\varepsilon>0$; compare~\cite{ChaudhuryGM20}), but perturbing makes every good relevant to every agent, and no result we know of implies Theorem~\ref{thm:target}.

\section{Overview}\label{sec:overview}

This section explains the approach in plain words; everything is defined and proved later.

\paragraph{Peeling.} If agent $i$ values its favourite remaining good $p$ at least as much as all its other remaining goods together, it may take $\{p\}$ and leave: it values every other bundle at most at $v_i(p)$, and nobody strongly envies a bundle of one good (Lemma~\ref{lem:peel}). We repeat this (\emph{rule R1}). When nobody can leave, every remaining agent values exactly three remaining goods $a\succ b\succ c$, with $v(a)<v(b)+v(c)$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:R1fail}): the \emph{core case}.

\paragraph{States.} In the core case, a \emph{state} lets each agent hold nothing, one of its goods, or its pair $\{b,c\}$; the other goods are \emph{leftover}. Utilities are ordinal: pair, then $a$, $b$, $c$, then nothing. An agent holding its pair is always safe. An agent holding one good is safe as long as the goods it ranks higher, its \emph{needs}, stay alone in their bundles, with one exception: an agent holding only $a$ is not safe if $b$ and $c$ lie together in a bundle of three or more goods (Lemma~\ref{lem:safe}). A state is \emph{valid} if every needed good is held alone. To finish, one agent, the \emph{absorber}, takes the leftovers. It must hold no needed good, and every agent $x$ that holds only its top and would find $b_x$ and $c_x$ in the absorber's bundle (an \emph{exposed} agent) must be protected by sending one of them to another free agent, one good each. If this is possible the state is \emph{completable}, and the result is \efxz{} (Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}).

\paragraph{Improving.} The draft gives a valid state, which may not be completable. The Improvement Lemma (Theorem~\ref{thm:improve}) says that then a valid state Pareto-dominates it, by a \emph{need cycle} (agents in a cycle take the good of the previous one, which they need), a \emph{pair chain} (an agent whose $b$ and $c$ are both leftover takes them, and its top travels along agents that need it), or an \emph{exchange cycle}, which mixes need arcs with \emph{exposure arcs} $o\to x$: $x$, exposed for the free agent $o$, takes its pair, made of $o$'s good and a leftover good. The key step is a count. If no free agent can absorb, each free agent has at least $|F|$ distinct protecting goods, where $F$ is the set of free agents, so distinct ones can be chosen for all free agents greedily. Each free agent then points to an exposed agent, every other agent to an agent that needs its good, and this map has a cycle. In the instance of Section~\ref{sec:example}, every improvement gives pairs to two agents at once; the one rotation of earlier algorithms is the case of one exposure arc (Section~\ref{sec:size}).

\section{Preliminaries}\label{sec:prelim}

\subsection{Model, threats and safety}\label{sec:model}

An \emph{instance} consists of a finite set $N$ of $n\ge1$ agents, a finite set $M$ of $m$ goods, and nonnegative additive valuations $v_i$, $i\in N$. Agents and goods are numbered, and ``index order'' and ``the first'' refer to these numbers. For a set $B$ of goods, the \emph{threat} of $B$ to agent $i$ is
\[
  \theta_i(B)\;=\;v_i(B)-\min_{h\in B}v_i(h)\quad\text{if } B\ne\emptyset,\qquad \theta_i(\emptyset)=0 .
\]
By additivity, $\theta_i(B)$ is the largest value of $v_i(B\setminus\{h\})$ over $h\in B$. Agent $i$ is \emph{safe} in an allocation $X$ if $v_i(X_i)\ge\theta_i(X_j)$ for every agent $j\ne i$. So $X$ is \efxz{} if and only if every agent is safe. A good is \emph{alone} in $X$ if it is the only good of its bundle.

\begin{lemma}[threats]\label{lem:threat}
For every agent $i$ and every set $B$ of goods:
(a) if $|B|\le1$, then $\theta_i(B)=0$;
(b) $\theta_i(B)\le v_i(B\cap R_i)$;
(c) $\theta_i(\{g,h\})=\max(v_i(g),v_i(h))$ for goods $g\ne h$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) $\theta_i(\{h\})=v_i(h)-v_i(h)=0$. (b) The minimum is at least $0$, and $v_i(B)=v_i(B\cap R_i)$ because the other goods of $B$ are worth $0$ to $i$. (c) $v_i(g)+v_i(h)-\min(v_i(g),v_i(h))=\max(v_i(g),v_i(h))$. \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent When $|R_i|=3$ we name agent $i$'s relevant goods $a_i$, $b_i$, $c_i$ so that $v_i(a_i)\ge v_i(b_i)\ge v_i(c_i)>0$, breaking ties between equal values by index. This gives a strict \emph{ranking} $a_i\succ_i b_i\succ_i c_i$, fixed once and for all; $g\succ_i h$ implies $v_i(g)\ge v_i(h)$. Agent $i$ is \emph{balanced} if $v_i(a_i)\le v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)$, and \emph{strictly balanced} if $v_i(a_i)<v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)$.

The next lemma is the half of the description of safety that the proofs need: it says when an agent with three relevant goods is certainly safe. (Section~\ref{sec:limits} gives the full description, which only the counterexamples there use.)

\begin{lemma}[safety]\label{lem:safe}
Let $X$ be an allocation and $i$ a balanced agent with $|R_i|=3$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] If $b_i,c_i\in X_i$, then $i$ is safe.
\item[(b)] Let $y\in X_i\cap R_i$, or $y=\bot$ (``no good''). Let $G_y=\{g\in R_i\st g\succ_i y\}$, and $G_\bot=R_i$. Suppose that no bundle $X_j$ with $j\ne i$ and $|X_j|\ge2$ contains a good of $G_y$. Then $i$ is safe, unless $y=a_i$ and some bundle $X_j$ with $j\ne i$ and $|X_j|\ge3$ contains both $b_i$ and $c_i$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Fix $j\ne i$. If $|X_j|\le1$, then $\theta_i(X_j)=0$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:threat}(a). So let $|X_j|\ge2$, and let $T=X_j\cap R_i$; then $\theta_i(X_j)\le v_i(T)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:threat}(b).

(a) $X_j$ is disjoint from $X_i$, which contains $b_i$ and $c_i$, so $T\subseteq\{a_i\}$ and $v_i(T)\le v_i(a_i)\le v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)\le v_i(X_i)$.

(b) If $y=\bot$, then $T\subseteq R_i=G_\bot$, so $T=\emptyset$ by hypothesis, and $\theta_i(X_j)\le0$. Otherwise $y\in X_i$. A good $g\in T$ is not in $G_y$ (by hypothesis) and is not $y$ (which lies in $X_i$, not in $X_j$), so $y\succ_i g$ and $v_i(g)\le v_i(y)$. If $|T|\le1$, then $v_i(T)\le v_i(y)\le v_i(X_i)$. If $|T|\ge2$, then $y$ and two goods ranked below it are three goods of $R_i$; as $|R_i|=3$, this forces $y=a_i$ and $T=\{b_i,c_i\}$. If $|X_j|=2$, then $X_j=\{b_i,c_i\}$ and $\theta_i(X_j)=v_i(b_i)\le v_i(a_i)\le v_i(X_i)$ by Lemma~\ref{lem:threat}(c). If $|X_j|\ge3$, we are in the exception. \qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Peeling}\label{sec:peel}

\begin{lemma}[peeling]\label{lem:peel}
Let $i\in N$ and $P\subseteq M$ satisfy
(i) $v_i(P)\ge v_i(M\setminus P)$, and
(ii) $|P|\le1$.
If the instance with agents $N\setminus\{i\}$ and goods $M\setminus P$ has an \efxz{} allocation $Z$, then $Z$ together with $X_i=P$ is an \efxz{} allocation of the whole instance.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Agent $i$: every other bundle $Z_j$ lies in $M\setminus P$, so $\theta_i(Z_j)\le v_i(Z_j)\le v_i(M\setminus P)\le v_i(P)$, using that the minimum in the threat is nonnegative, and (i). An agent $j\ne i$ towards $P$: $\theta_j(P)=0$ by (ii) and Lemma~\ref{lem:threat}(a). An agent $j\ne i$ towards $Z_k$ with $k\ne i,j$: this is a constraint of $Z$, and $j$ still holds $Z_j$. \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent \emph{Rule R1.} Let $G$ be the set of remaining goods, and let $p$ be agent $i$'s favourite good of $G$ (largest value, ties by index). If $v_i(p)=0$, so that $i$ values no good of $G$, then $i$ may leave with $P=\emptyset$, and (i) reads $0\ge0$. If $v_i(p)>0$ and $v_i(G\setminus\{p\})\le v_i(p)$, then $i$ may leave with $P=\{p\}$. Rule R1 applies, for instance, to every agent that values at most two remaining goods, and to every agent with $v_i(a_i)\ge v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)$.

\begin{corollary}[two relevant goods]\label{cor:k2}
If $|R_i|\le2$ for every agent, then serial dictatorship, in any order of the agents, each taking a favourite remaining good (or nothing once no good is left), with the last agent taking all remaining goods, returns an \efxz{} allocation.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By induction on the number of agents. One agent faces no constraint. Otherwise the first agent $i$ values at most two remaining goods, so rule R1 applies to its choice: its other relevant good, if any, is worth at most the good it takes. The rest of the procedure is serial dictatorship on the smaller instance, which is \efxz{} by induction, and Lemma~\ref{lem:peel} finishes the proof. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[when R1 applies to nobody]\label{lem:R1fail}
Let agent $i$ value at most three goods of the set $G$ of remaining goods, and suppose that rule R1 does not apply to $i$. Then $i$ values exactly three goods of $G$ and is strictly balanced with respect to them.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Rule R1 fails, so $v_i(p)>0$ and $v_i(G\setminus\{p\})>v_i(p)$ for the favourite $p$. If $i$ valued at most two goods of $G$, then $v_i(G\setminus\{p\})$ would be the value of at most one good, which is at most $v_i(p)$. So $i$ values exactly three goods of $G$, $p$ is its top $a_i$, and $v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)=v_i(G\setminus\{p\})>v_i(a_i)$. \qed
\end{proof}

\section{States and Soundness}\label{sec:states}

In this section and the next we are in the \emph{core case}: every agent values exactly three goods and is strictly balanced. (Strict balance is not used below: the order of utilities is a bookkeeping device, and soundness needs only balance.) Goods that no agent values may exist. In examples, $g_0,g_1,\dots$ are goods.

\subsection{Definitions}\label{sec:defs}

\begin{definition}[states]\label{def:state}
A \emph{state} $Y$ gives each agent $i$ a \emph{holding} $Y_i$, one of $\emptyset$, $\{a_i\}$, $\{b_i\}$, $\{c_i\}$ and $\{b_i,c_i\}$ (its \emph{pair}), with the holdings pairwise disjoint.
\begin{itemize}
\item $U=\{i\st Y_i=\{b_i,c_i\}\}$ is the set of \emph{pair holders}.
\item $J=M\setminus\bigcup_iY_i$ is the set of \emph{leftover} goods (\emph{junk}).
\item For $i\notin U$ with $|Y_i|=1$, write $Y_i=\{y_i\}$.
\item The \emph{utility} of $i$ is $u_i(Y)=4,3,2,1,0$ if $Y_i$ is its pair, $\{a_i\}$, $\{b_i\}$, $\{c_i\}$, $\emptyset$. Since $v_i(a_i)<v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)$, a larger utility never means a smaller value.
\item A state $Y'$ \emph{Pareto-dominates} $Y$ if $u_i(Y')\ge u_i(Y)$ for every $i$, with strict inequality for some $i$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[needs, validity, free agents]\label{def:valid}
Let $Y$ be a state.
\begin{itemize}
\item The \emph{needs} of agent $i$ are: $N_i=\emptyset$ if $i\in U$; $N_i=R_i$ if $Y_i=\emptyset$; and $N_i=\{g\in R_i\st g\succ_i y_i\}$ if $Y_i=\{y_i\}$. ($N$ without a subscript is the set of agents.) No agent needs a good it holds. The set of goods \emph{needed alone} is $\NA=\bigcup_iN_i$.
\item $Y$ is \emph{valid} if every $g\in\NA$ is the only good of some agent: $Y_j=\{g\}$ for some $j$. Such a $j$ is not in $U$, and it is not an agent that needs $g$.
\item An agent $i\notin U$ is \emph{free} if $Y_i=\emptyset$ or $y_i\notin\NA$. $F$ is the set of free agents. An agent outside $U\cup F$ holds a single good that some agent needs.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\noindent In a valid state, the leftover goods and the goods of pair holders are not needed: every needed good is held alone.

\begin{definition}[need digraph]\label{def:D}
The \emph{need digraph} $D$ of a state has the agents outside $U$ as vertices, and an arc $j\to j'$ (a \emph{need arc}) whenever $j$ holds a single good $y_j$ and $y_j\in N_{j'}$. It has no loops, since no agent needs its own good.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[exposure, absorbers, completions]\label{def:absorb}
Let $Y$ be a state and $o$ an agent.
\begin{itemize}
\item An agent $x$ is \emph{exposed for $o$} if $x\ne o$, $x\notin U$, $Y_x=\{a_x\}$, and $b_x,c_x\in J\cup Y_o$. $E_o$ is the set of agents exposed for $o$.
\item $o$ is a \emph{valid absorber}, with a set $H$, if $o\in F\cup U$ and
 (A1) $\{b_x,c_x\}\cap J\ne\emptyset$ for every $x\in E_o$, and
 (A2) $H\subseteq J$ meets $\{b_x,c_x\}\cap J$ for every $x\in E_o$, and $|H|\le|F\setminus\{o\}|$.
 The goods of $H$ are the \emph{protecting goods}. $Y$ is \emph{completable} if some agent is a valid absorber.
\item The \emph{completion} $X$ of $Y$ with a valid absorber $o$ and its set $H$: each good of $H$ goes to a different free agent other than $o$ (there are enough, by (A2)); $o$ receives $X_o=Y_o\cup(J\setminus H)$; every other agent $i$ keeps $X_i=Y_i$, or $X_i=Y_i\cup\{h\}$ if it received $h\in H$.
\end{itemize}
\end{definition}

\noindent The completion is an allocation of all goods, since $M$ is the disjoint union of the holdings and $J$.

\subsection{Soundness}\label{sec:sound}

\begin{theorem}[soundness]\label{thm:sound}
Let $Y$ be a valid state, $o$ a valid absorber with set $H$, and $X$ the completion. Then $X$ is \efxz{}, and every bundle other than $X_o$ has at most two goods.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Sizes.} A free agent other than $o$ holds at most one good and receives at most one good of $H$. A pair holder other than $o$ holds two goods. Every other agent $j\ne o$ is outside $U\cup F$ and keeps its single good. So only $X_o$ can have three or more goods.

\emph{(N) Needed goods stay alone.} Let $g\in\NA$. By validity, $Y_j=\{g\}$ for some agent $j$. Then $j\notin U$, and $j\notin F$ because $g$ is needed. So $j\ne o$, as $o\in F\cup U$, and $j$ receives no good of $H$, which goes to free agents. Hence $X_j=\{g\}$.

\emph{(OC) The owner constraint.} No agent $x\ne o$ with $x\notin U$ and $Y_x=\{a_x\}$ has both $b_x$ and $c_x$ in $X_o$. Indeed, suppose it had. Since $X_o=Y_o\cup(J\setminus H)$, we get $b_x,c_x\in J\cup Y_o$, so $x\in E_o$. By (A2), $H$ contains a good of $\{b_x,c_x\}\cap J$. That good went to an agent other than $o$, and it is not in $Y_o$ (it is leftover), so it is not in $X_o$: a contradiction.

\emph{Safety.} Let $i$ be an agent; it is balanced. If $i\in U$, then $b_i,c_i\in Y_i\subseteq X_i$, and $i$ is safe by Lemma~\ref{lem:safe}(a). Let $i\notin U$. Apply Lemma~\ref{lem:safe}(b) with $y=y_i$ if $Y_i=\{y_i\}$ (then $y\in Y_i\subseteq X_i$), and $y=\bot$ if $Y_i=\emptyset$. Then $G_y=N_i\subseteq\NA$, so by (N) every good of $G_y$ is alone, and no bundle of two or more goods contains one. So $i$ is safe unless $y_i=a_i$ and some $X_j$ with $j\ne i$ and $|X_j|\ge3$ contains $b_i$ and $c_i$. By the sizes, $j=o$; so $i\ne o$, $i\notin U$, $Y_i=\{a_i\}$ and $b_i,c_i\in X_o$, which (OC) excludes. \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent The proof uses only validity, the definition of a valid absorber, and that pair holders are balanced. Nothing in it depends on how the state was reached.

\subsection{The draft, and two basic facts}\label{sec:draft}

\begin{lemma}[the draft]\label{lem:draft}
Let the agents, in any order, each take the first of $a_i$, $b_i$, $c_i$ not yet taken, or nothing if all three are taken. The result is a valid state with $U=\emptyset$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The holdings are single goods of $R_i$ or empty, and no good is taken twice, so this is a state, and $U=\emptyset$. Let $g\in N_i$. Then $i$ took a good ranked below $g$, or nothing, so $g$ was already taken when $i$ chose. It was taken by an earlier agent $j$, as its only good, and holdings never change during the draft. So $Y_j=\{g\}$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[two basic facts]\label{lem:facts}
Let $Y$ and $Y'$ be states, with needs $N_i$ and $N'_i$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(F1)] If $u_i(Y')\ge u_i(Y)$, then $N'_i\subseteq N_i$.
\item[(F2)] If $Y$ is valid, every agent outside $U\cup F$ has an out-arc in $D$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(F1) If $Y'_i$ is the pair, $N'_i=\emptyset$. If $Y'_i=\{g'\}$, then $u_i(Y)\le u_i(Y')<4$, so $Y_i$ is not the pair: either $Y_i=\emptyset$ and $N_i=R_i\supseteq N'_i$, or $Y_i=\{g\}$ with $g'=g$ or $g'\succ_ig$, and then every good ranked above $g'$ is ranked above $g$. If $Y'_i=\emptyset$, then $u_i(Y)=0$, so $Y_i=\emptyset$ and $N'_i=N_i$.
(F2) Let $j\notin U\cup F$. Then $j$ holds a single good $y_j\in\NA$, so $y_j\in N_{j'}$ for some agent $j'$. We have $j'\ne j$, since no agent needs its own good, and $j'\notin U$, since pair holders need nothing. So $j\to j'$ is an arc of $D$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[transfer]\label{lem:transfer}
Let $Y$ be valid, and let $Y'$ be a state such that
(a) $u_i(Y')\ge u_i(Y)$ for every agent $i$, with strict inequality for some $i$, and
(b) every $g\in\NA$ is, in $Y'$, the only good of some agent.
Then $Y'$ is valid and Pareto-dominates $Y$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By (a) and (F1), $\NA'\subseteq\NA$. By (b), every good of $\NA'$ is the only good of some agent in $Y'$. Domination is (a). \qed
\end{proof}

\section{The Improvement Lemma}\label{sec:improve}

\begin{theorem}[Improvement Lemma]\label{thm:improve}
In the core case, let $Y$ be a valid state in which no free agent is a valid absorber and some agent is not a pair holder. Then some valid state Pareto-dominates $Y$. It is obtained from $Y$ by one need cycle, pair chain or exchange cycle (defined below), which can be found in polynomial time.
\end{theorem}

\noindent If $Y$ is not completable, then no free agent is a valid absorber, and not every agent is a pair holder (otherwise every agent is a valid absorber with $H=\emptyset$: nobody is exposed, because exposed agents are outside $U$). So the theorem applies to every valid state that is not completable.

\begin{corollary}[Pareto-optimal states]\label{cor:po}
In the core case, every valid state that no valid state Pareto-dominates is completable, with a free absorber, unless every agent holds its pair.
\end{corollary}

\noindent Valid states exist (Lemma~\ref{lem:draft}), there are finitely many, and a valid state with the largest sum of utilities is not dominated by any valid state. So Corollary~\ref{cor:po} and Theorem~\ref{thm:sound} already prove Theorem~\ref{thm:target} in the core case; \DE{} (Section~\ref{sec:algo}) finds such a state by exchanges.

The proof is split into the lemmas below. Throughout, $Y$ is valid.

\subsection{Need cycles and pair chains}\label{sec:P}

\begin{lemma}[need cycle; pair chain]\label{lem:P}
(a) If $D$ has a cycle, $Y$ is dominated by a valid state. (b) If some agent $x\notin U$ holds only its top $a_x$, and $b_x,c_x\in J$, then $Y$ is dominated by a valid state.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
(a) \emph{Need cycle.} Let $j_0\to j_1\to\dots\to j_{k-1}\to j_0$ be a cycle of $D$, with $k\ge2$ distinct agents. Each of them is the tail of an arc, so it is outside $U$ and holds a single good. Define $Y'$: each $j_{t+1}$ takes $y_{j_t}$ (indices modulo $k$), and every other agent keeps its holding. Each $y_{j_t}$ is a good of $R_{j_{t+1}}$, so $Y'_{j_{t+1}}$ is an allowed holding. The goods of the cycle are permuted, so the holdings are disjoint. Each $j_{t+1}$ receives a good that it needs, so it strictly improves; the other agents are unchanged. This is condition (a) of Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer}. For (b): a needed good $g$ is $Y_j=\{g\}$ for some $j$; if $j$ is off the cycle, it keeps $g$ alone, and if $j=j_t$, then $j_{t+1}$ holds $g$ alone. Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer} applies.

(b) \emph{Pair chain.} Walk in $D$: $j_0=x$, and while $j_t$ has an out-arc, let $j_{t+1}$ be an out-neighbour. If the walk repeats an agent, $D$ has a cycle, and (a) applies. Otherwise it stops at an agent $j_k$ ($k\ge0$) with no out-arc. Define $Y'$: $x$ takes its pair $\{b_x,c_x\}$; for $1\le t\le k$, $j_t$ takes $y_{j_{t-1}}$, where $y_{j_0}=a_x$; every other agent keeps its holding. The old good of $j_k$ ($a_x$ if $k=0$), if any, becomes leftover. Every $j_t$ with $t<k$ has an out-arc, so it holds a single good. The holdings are disjoint: $b_x$ and $c_x$ were leftover, and the goods $y_{j_0},\dots,y_{j_{k-1}}$ each move one step along the path. Agent $x$ goes from $a_x$ to its pair, and each $j_t$ with $t\ge1$ receives a good it needs: (a) of Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer}. For (b): $b_x$ and $c_x$ are leftover, so they are not needed, by validity. A needed good held by an agent off the path stays alone. The good $y_{j_t}$ with $t<k$ moves to $j_{t+1}$, alone. Finally, $j_k$ is outside $U$ ($j_0=x\notin U$, and for $k\ge1$, $j_k$ needs something) and has no out-arc, so $j_k\in F$ by (F2): its old good, if any, is not needed. Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer} applies. \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent From now on we assume

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{(P)} no agent $x\notin U$ with $Y_x=\{a_x\}$ has both $b_x$ and $c_x$ in $J$,

\smallskip
\noindent since otherwise Lemma~\ref{lem:P}(b) applies. We do not assume that $D$ is acyclic; the cycles found below may be cycles of $D$.

\subsection{Free agents and protecting goods}\label{sec:free}

\begin{lemma}[an agent holding nothing absorbs]\label{lem:empty}
Assume (P). If $o\in F$ holds nothing, then $E_o=\emptyset$, and $o$ is a valid absorber with $H=\emptyset$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
An agent $x\in E_o$ would have $b_x,c_x\in J\cup Y_o=J$, against (P). With $E_o=\emptyset$, (A1) and (A2) hold for $H=\emptyset$. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[protecting goods are forced]\label{lem:forced}
Assume (P), and let $o\in F$ hold the single good $y_o$. Every $x\in E_o$ has $\{b_x,c_x\}=\{y_o,h_x\}$ for a leftover good $h_x\in J$, its \emph{protecting good}. Let $H_o=\{h_x\st x\in E_o\}$. Then $o$ is a valid absorber if and only if $|H_o|\le|F|-1$, and then $H=H_o$ is a valid set.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $x\in E_o$, $b_x,c_x\in J\cup\{y_o\}$, and not both are in $J$, by (P). As $b_x\ne c_x$, exactly one of them is $y_o$, and the other, $h_x$, is in $J$. So (A1) holds, and $\{b_x,c_x\}\cap J=\{h_x\}$. A set $H\subseteq J$ meets all these singletons if and only if $H\supseteq H_o$. So (A2) can be met if and only if $|H_o|\le|F\setminus\{o\}|=|F|-1$, and then by $H=H_o$. \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent The protecting good $h_x$ depends only on $x$: under (P), an agent $x$ lies in $E_o$ for at most one free agent $o$ that holds a good. Indeed, if $x\in E_o\cap E_{o'}$ with $o\ne o'$, then $\{b_x,c_x\}$ contains $y_o$ and $y_{o'}$, two different goods that are not leftover, so $\{b_x,c_x\}=\{y_o,y_{o'}\}$ contains no leftover good, against Lemma~\ref{lem:forced}.

\begin{lemma}[no free agent]\label{lem:noF}
If $F=\emptyset$, then either every agent is in $U$, and then every agent is a valid absorber with $H=\emptyset$, or $D$ has a cycle.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If every agent is in $U$, nobody is exposed (exposed agents are outside $U$), so (A1) and (A2) hold with $H=\emptyset$. Otherwise $D$ has a vertex, and every vertex is outside $U\cup F$, so it has an out-arc by (F2), to another vertex. A finite nonempty digraph in which every vertex has an out-arc has a cycle. \qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Exchange cycles}\label{sec:exchange}

\begin{definition}[exchange digraph]\label{def:Dplus}
Assume (P). The \emph{exchange digraph} $\Dp$ has the agents outside $U$ as vertices, the need arcs of $D$, and an \emph{exposure arc} $o\to x$ for every free agent $o$ that holds a good and every $x\in E_o$; this arc carries the \emph{label} $h_x$.
\end{definition}

\noindent The head of an exposure arc holds its top, so it needs nothing; the head of a need arc needs something. So the kind of an arc is determined by its head.

\begin{definition}[exchange along a cycle]\label{def:exchange}
Let $C=(w_0,w_1,\dots,w_{k-1})$ be a cycle of $\Dp$ with distinct agents and $k\ge2$, indices modulo $k$. The \emph{exchange along $C$} is the state $Y'$ in which, for every $t$:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $w_t\to w_{t+1}$ is an exposure arc, $w_{t+1}$ takes its pair $\{b_{w_{t+1}},c_{w_{t+1}}\}=\{y_{w_t},h_{w_{t+1}}\}$;
\item if $w_t\to w_{t+1}$ is a need arc, $w_{t+1}$ takes $y_{w_t}$ alone;
\end{itemize}
and every agent off $C$ keeps its holding. A need cycle is the case without exposure arcs.
\end{definition}

\noindent Every agent of $C$ holds a single good: the tail of a need arc holds a single good by definition, and the tail of an exposure arc is a free agent that holds a good. Each agent of $C$ has exactly one predecessor on $C$, so $Y'$ is well defined.

\begin{lemma}[exchange cycle]\label{lem:exchange}
Assume (P). Let $C$ be a cycle of $\Dp$ whose exposure arcs have pairwise distinct labels. Then the exchange along $C$ is a valid state that Pareto-dominates $Y$, in which exactly the heads of the exposure arcs of $C$ become new pair holders.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Disjoint holdings.} Each good $y_{w_t}$ moves to $w_{t+1}$ only. The labels of the exposure arcs of $C$ are leftover goods, so nobody holds them in $Y$, and they are pairwise distinct by hypothesis. The agents off $C$ keep goods that are neither leftover nor held by agents of $C$. So the holdings of $Y'$ are disjoint, and each is an allowed holding: a pair, or a single good of $R_{w_{t+1}}$ (a needed good).

\emph{Improvement.} The head of an exposure arc holds only its top in $Y$ and its pair in $Y'$: utility $3$ to $4$. The head of a need arc receives a good it needs, which it ranks above its old good (or it held nothing): its utility strictly increases. The agents off $C$ are unchanged. This is (a) of Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer}.

\emph{Needed goods.} Let $g\in\NA$, with $Y_j=\{g\}$. If $j\notin C$, then $j$ keeps $g$ alone. If $j=w_t$, then $w_t$ is not free (its good is needed), so the arc $w_t\to w_{t+1}$ is a need arc, and $w_{t+1}$ holds $g$ alone. This is (b) of Lemma~\ref{lem:transfer}, which gives validity and domination. The new pair holders are the heads of the exposure arcs. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[an exchange cycle exists]\label{lem:cycle}
Assume (P). Suppose that $F\ne\emptyset$, that no free agent holds nothing, and that $|H_o|\ge|F|$ for every $o\in F$. Then $\Dp$ has a cycle whose exposure arcs have pairwise distinct labels; so $Y$ is dominated by a valid state.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Distinct representatives.} List $F=\{o_1,\dots,o_f\}$. For $i=1,\dots,f$ in turn, pick $q_{o_i}\in H_{o_i}\setminus\{q_{o_1},\dots,q_{o_{i-1}}\}$. This is possible because $|H_{o_i}|\ge f>i-1$. Let $x_{o_i}\in E_{o_i}$ be an agent whose protecting good is $q_{o_i}$. (The agents $x_o$ are then distinct, since $h_x$ depends only on $x$; the argument below does not need this.)

\emph{A functional digraph.} Define $\sigma$ on the agents outside $U$: $\sigma(o)=x_o$ for $o\in F$, and $\sigma(j)=$ an out-neighbour of $j$ in $D$ for $j\notin U\cup F$, which exists by (F2). Then $\sigma(j)\notin U$ and $\sigma(j)\ne j$ for every $j$: exposed agents are outside $U$ and differ from $o$, and $D$ has no loops. Every arc $j\to\sigma(j)$ is an arc of $\Dp$: an exposure arc if $j\in F$, a need arc otherwise. The agents outside $U$ form a nonempty set (it contains $F$), so following $\sigma$ from any of them must repeat an agent, which gives a cycle $C$ of $\Dp$ with $k\ge2$ distinct agents. Its exposure arcs are arcs $o\to x_o$ for distinct free agents $o$, with the distinct labels $h_o$. Lemma~\ref{lem:exchange} finishes the proof. \qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Proof of the Improvement Lemma}\label{sec:proofimprove}

\begin{proof}[of Theorem~\ref{thm:improve}]
If (P) fails, Lemma~\ref{lem:P}(b) gives a dominating valid state (a pair chain, or a need cycle). Assume (P). If $F=\emptyset$, then not every agent is in $U$, so Lemma~\ref{lem:noF} gives a cycle of $D$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:P}(a) gives a dominating valid state. Otherwise $F\ne\emptyset$, and no free agent is a valid absorber. By Lemma~\ref{lem:empty}, no free agent holds nothing. By Lemma~\ref{lem:forced}, $|H_o|\ge|F|$ for every $o\in F$. Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle} gives a dominating valid state.

Each step is a walk in a digraph with at most $n$ vertices, or a greedy choice among at most $m$ goods per free agent, so the dominating state is found in polynomial time. \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[of Corollary~\ref{cor:po}]
Let $Y$ be valid and not dominated by any valid state. If every agent holds its pair, $Y$ is completable (Lemma~\ref{lem:noF}). Otherwise, if no free agent were a valid absorber, Theorem~\ref{thm:improve} would give a dominating valid state. \qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{Remarks}\label{sec:remarks}

\paragraph{The dichotomy is sharp.} Under (P), a free agent holding a good fails as an absorber exactly when $|H_o|\ge|F|$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:forced}), and the count is used only to choose distinct protecting goods. The proof never uses a pair holder as the absorber, except when every agent holds its pair.

\paragraph{The protecting goods cannot be dropped.} Even in a Pareto-optimal valid state, every free agent may have an exposed agent, so the protecting goods are needed. Take two agents with rankings $g_0\succ g_1\succ g_2$ and $g_1\succ g_0\succ g_2$, each holding its top. Both are free, $J=\{g_2\}$, and each is exposed for the other. No valid state dominates this one (each agent's pair contains the other's top), and it is completable only with $H=\{g_2\}$ (\cite{RepoS}, \file{k3/simplify/po/hall/NOTES.md}, \S6).

\paragraph{A second proof.}\label{sec:second} A second, independently found proof of Corollary~\ref{cor:po} (\cite{RepoS}, \file{k3/simplify/po/potential/NOTES.md}) shows that a valid state with no need cycle, pair chain or exchange cycle is completable, by a walk instead of a choice of all representatives at once. It leaves each free agent by an exposure arc whose label has not been used yet (each free agent has at least $|F|$ labels, and fewer have been used), then follows need arcs, in the acyclic $D$, to a free agent. The walk returns to a free agent it has visited, and the shortest closed part of it is a cycle of $\Dp$ with distinct labels (a \emph{rainbow} cycle). Both proofs first apply pair chains and let an agent holding nothing absorb.

\section{The Algorithm Draft and Exchange}\label{sec:algo}

\subsection{The algorithm}\label{sec:pseudo}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:de} lists \DE{}. It receives the values $v_i(g)$; ties among an agent's values are broken by index, as in Section~\ref{sec:model}. The loop of lines~\ref{l:loop}--\ref{l:end} runs on the core case left by peeling, with the rankings and the state of Section~\ref{sec:states}.

\begin{algorithm}[tb]
\caption{Draft and Exchange (\DE{})}\label{alg:de}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require agents $N$, goods $M$, nonnegative values $v_i(g)$, every agent with $|R_i|\le3$
\State $A\gets N$, $G\gets M$
\While{$|A|\ge2$ and rule R1 applies to some agent of $A$ with respect to $G$} \Comment{peeling}
  \State $i\gets$ the first such agent; $X_i\gets P_i$ (by rule R1)
  \State $A\gets A\setminus\{i\}$, $G\gets G\setminus P_i$
\EndWhile
\If{$|A|=1$} give $G$ to the agent of $A$ and \Return $X$ \EndIf
\State \Comment{core case (Lemma~\ref{lem:R1fail})}
\For{$i\in A$ in index order} $Y_i\gets$ the first of $\{a_i\},\{b_i\},\{c_i\}$ whose good is not yet taken, else $\emptyset$ \EndFor
\Loop\label{l:loop}
  \If{some $x\notin U$ has $Y_x=\{a_x\}$ and $b_x,c_x\in J$}
    \State apply the pair chain, or need cycle, of Lemma~\ref{lem:P}(b)
  \ElsIf{every agent of $A$ is in $U$}
    \State $o\gets$ the first agent of $A$; $H\gets\emptyset$; \textbf{break}
  \ElsIf{some $o\in F$ has $Y_o=\emptyset$}
    \State $o\gets$ the first such agent; $H\gets\emptyset$; \textbf{break} \Comment{Lemma~\ref{lem:empty}}
  \ElsIf{some $o\in F$ has $|H_o|\le|F|-1$}
    \State $o\gets$ the first such agent; $H\gets H_o$; \textbf{break} \Comment{Lemma~\ref{lem:forced}}
  \ElsIf{$F=\emptyset$}
    \State apply a need cycle, found by following out-arcs of $D$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:noF})
  \Else
    \State exchange along the cycle of $\sigma$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle})
  \EndIf
\EndLoop\label{l:end}
\State give each good of $H$ to a different free agent other than $o$, in index order
\State $X_o\gets Y_o\cup(J\setminus H)$; every other $i\in A$ keeps $Y_i$ (plus its good of $H$, if any)
\State \Return $X$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The loop tests for an absorber only when (P) holds. There the test is a count (Lemma~\ref{lem:forced}). Without (P) the test would ask for a smallest set meeting given sets of one or two goods, which we never need: a pair chain is applied first.

\subsection{Correctness and running time}\label{sec:correct}

\begin{theorem}[\DE{} is correct]\label{thm:de}
On every instance in which every agent values at most three goods, \DE{} returns a complete \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles but at most one have at most two goods. It peels at most $n$ agents and applies at most $4n$ exchanges (need cycles, pair chains and exchange cycles).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Peeling.} Each round of the first loop removes one agent, so there are at most $n$ rounds. Let $i_1,\dots,i_p$ be the peeled agents, in order, with sets $P_1,\dots,P_p$, and let $(A_s,G_s)$ be the agents and goods left after $s$ rounds. Rule R1 for $i_{s}$ in $(A_{s-1},G_{s-1})$ is conditions (i) and (ii) of Lemma~\ref{lem:peel}. So if $(A_s,G_s)$ has an \efxz{} allocation, so has $(A_{s-1},G_{s-1})$, with $X_{i_s}=P_s$ added. By induction from $s=p$ down to $s=1$, it suffices that the allocation that \DE{} builds for $(A_p,G_p)$ is \efxz{}. The loop stops with $|A_p|\ge1$. If $|A_p|=1$, the single agent takes $G_p$ and faces no other bundle.

\emph{The core.} Let $|A_p|\ge2$. Rule R1 applies to no agent of $A_p$, so by Lemma~\ref{lem:R1fail} every agent of $A_p$ values exactly three goods of $G_p$ and is strictly balanced: the core case, on agents $A_p$ and goods $G_p$. The draft gives a valid state (Lemma~\ref{lem:draft}). Each branch of the loop that does not stop replaces the state by a valid state that Pareto-dominates it (Lemmas~\ref{lem:P}, \ref{lem:noF} and~\ref{lem:cycle}; Lemma~\ref{lem:cycle} applies because the earlier branches did not fire, as in the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:improve}). So the state stays valid, and the sum of utilities $\sum_{i\in A_p}u_i$, an integer between $0$ and $4|A_p|$, grows by at least $1$ with each exchange. Hence there are at most $4|A_p|\le4n$ exchanges, and the loop stops. When it stops, $o$ is a valid absorber with set $H$: by Lemma~\ref{lem:noF} if every agent is in $U$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:empty}, or by Lemma~\ref{lem:forced}. The last two lines build the completion, which is \efxz{} by Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}, and in which only $X_o$ can have more than two goods.

\emph{Shape.} Peeled agents receive at most one good. If $|A_p|=1$, the last agent's bundle is the only one that can be large; otherwise only the absorber's. \qed
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Running time.} Each iteration of the loop needs the holder of each good, the needs (at most three goods per agent), the free agents, the sets $E_o$ and $H_o$, and a walk of at most $n$ steps. Every agent $x$ that holds only its top meets at most one condition among: $b_x$ and $c_x$ both leftover (a pair chain), or exactly one of them leftover and the other held alone by a free agent $o$ (then $x\in E_o$, with $h_x$ the leftover one); so all sets $E_o$ and $H_o$ are found by one pass over the agents. With an array that records the holder of each good, an iteration takes $O(n+m)$ steps, and peeling takes $O(n)$ steps per round once the relevant goods of each agent are known. So \DE{} runs in $O(n(n+m))$ steps after reading the input. We have not formalized this count.

\begin{proof}[of Theorems~\ref{thm:target}, \ref{thm:D} and~\ref{thm:algo}]
By Theorem~\ref{thm:de}, \DE{} returns, on every instance in which every agent values at most three goods, a complete \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles but at most one have at most two goods: Theorems~\ref{thm:target} and~\ref{thm:D} (an agent that values exactly three goods values at most three). Theorem~\ref{thm:de} also bounds the peeling steps by $n$ and the exchanges by $4n$, and each step takes polynomial time, as just discussed: Theorem~\ref{thm:algo}. Every decision of \DE{} compares one value of an agent with another, or with the sum of two of its values (rule R1, the rankings), and the proofs use nothing else about the values; so they hold for nonnegative real values, with the step count of Theorem~\ref{thm:algo} in the comparison model just described. \qed
\end{proof}

\subsection{How large must an exchange be?}\label{sec:size}

\paragraph{The rotation is the case of one exposure arc.} K3ALG~\cite{LinK3} and its simplification K3S (\cite{RepoS}, \file{proofs/k3s_standalone.md}) end with at most one rotation: an agent $k$ exposed for the candidate absorber $r$ takes its pair, and its top travels along a chain of agents that need it, ending at $r$. This is an exchange cycle $r\to k\to\dots\to r$ with one exposure arc (or a pair chain when $b_k$, $c_k$ are leftover). After their specific draft, with priorities and blocks, one rotation suffices. After an arbitrary draft it does not: in Section~\ref{sec:example} every improvement upgrades two agents, so the local search that applies single upgrades, need cycles and one-arc rotations gets stuck there in a state that is not completable (\cite{RepoS}, \file{attempts/k3s-stuck-lemma.md}).

Call a \emph{short move} a need cycle, a pair chain, or an exchange along a cycle of $\Dp$ with exactly one exposure arc. The next proposition (a written proof, not refereed), from~\cite{RepoS} (\file{k3/simplify/po/potential/NOTES.md}, Lemma~3), shows that the instance of Section~\ref{sec:example} is as small as possible in its number of agents.

\begin{proposition}[when short moves are not enough]\label{prop:short}
In the core case, let $Y$ be a valid state in which no free agent is a valid absorber and some agent is not a pair holder, and to which no short move applies. Then $|F|\ge2$, $n\ge6$ and $m\ge8$; if $|F|\ge3$, then $n\ge9$ and $m\ge12$. Both bounds $n=6$ and $m=8$ are attained.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
No pair chain applies, so (P) holds (Lemma~\ref{lem:P}(b)). No need cycle applies, so $D$ is acyclic, and every walk along need arcs ends at an agent with no out-arc, which is free by (F2). By Lemma~\ref{lem:noF}, $F\ne\emptyset$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:empty}, every free agent holds a good, and by Lemma~\ref{lem:forced}, $|H_o|\ge|F|$ for every $o\in F$. Every agent holds at least one good: pair holders hold two, other agents outside $F$ hold a needed good, and free agents hold a good. Let $k=|F|$.

If $k=1$, let $F=\{o\}$. As $|H_o|\ge1$, some $x\in E_o$ exists, and $x\ne o$, so $x$ is not free. The walk along need arcs from $x$ ends at a free agent, that is, at $o$. Since $D$ is acyclic, $o\to x\to\dots\to o$ is a cycle of $\Dp$ with one exposure arc: a short move (Lemma~\ref{lem:exchange}). So $k\ge2$.

The sets $E_o$, $o\in F$, are pairwise disjoint (the remark after Lemma~\ref{lem:forced}), and $|E_o|\ge|H_o|\ge k$.

If $k=2$, let $F=\{o_1,o_2\}$ and $x\in E_{o_1}$. The walk along need arcs from $x$ cannot end at $o_1$, since that would give a short move as above; so it ends at $o_2$. As $|E_{o_1}|\ge2$, some $x\in E_{o_1}$ differs from $o_2$; this $x$ is not free, so its walk enters $o_2$ by a need arc, and $o_2$ needs a good. Then $o_2$ does not hold only its top, so $o_2\notin E_{o_1}$, and $E_{o_1}$ consists of at least two agents outside $F$. Symmetrically for $E_{o_2}$. So $n\ge2+2+2=6$. At least $n$ goods are held and $|J|\ge|H_{o_1}|\ge2$, so $m\ge8$.

If $k\ge3$, the disjoint sets $E_o$ contain at least $k^2$ agents, of which at most $k$ are free, so $n\ge k+(k^2-k)=k^2\ge9$, and $m\ge n+|J|\ge k^2+k\ge12$.

\emph{Attained.} The instance of Section~\ref{sec:example} has $n=6$ (and $m=10$). An instance with $n=6$ and $m=8$ is: $o_1$ ranks $g_4\succ g_5\succ g_0$, $o_2$ ranks $g_6\succ g_7\succ g_1$, $x_1$ ranks $g_4\succ g_1\succ g_2$, $x_1'$ ranks $g_5\succ g_3\succ g_1$, $x_2$ ranks $g_6\succ g_0\succ g_2$, $x_2'$ ranks $g_7\succ g_3\succ g_0$, with $o_1$ holding $g_0$, $o_2$ holding $g_1$, and every $x$ holding its top. Then $J=\{g_2,g_3\}$, $F=\{o_1,o_2\}$, $E_{o_2}=\{x_1,x_1'\}$ with protecting goods $g_2,g_3$, and $E_{o_1}=\{x_2,x_2'\}$ with protecting goods $g_2,g_3$; no free agent absorbs, and no short move applies (checked by our script). \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent In the second instance the distinct labels matter. Of the four cycles $o_2\to x\to o_1\to x'\to o_2$ with two exposure arcs, two use the same protecting good twice, and exchanging along them would give $g_2$ (or $g_3$) to two agents; the other two give valid dominating states (checked by our script).

\paragraph{No bounded number of exposure arcs suffices.} The second proof's notes~\cite{RepoS} (\S7) give valid states with $k$ free agents in a ring: $o_i$ needs the goods at the roots of a binary tree of agents that need their children's goods, and the leaves of the tree of $o_{i+1}$ hold their tops and are exposed for $o_i$. No free agent absorbs, and every cycle of $\Dp$ with distinct labels uses exactly $k$ exposure arcs. This was checked by computer for $(k,\text{depth})\in\{(2,1),(3,2),(4,2),(5,3)\}$ in the notes and for $k\le4$ by our script; the referee ran \DE{} on rings with $k\le9$. We have not proved that every Pareto improvement there upgrades $k$ agents.

\section{Limits of the Shape}\label{sec:limits}

Could every instance have an \efxz{} allocation with all bundles of at most two goods, or with one bundle of exactly three? No. The proof uses the full description of safety for agents with distinct values.

\begin{lemma}[cases of safety]\label{lem:L5}
Let agent $i$ have $R_i=\{a,b,c\}$ with $v_i(a)>v_i(b)>v_i(c)>0$ and $v_i(a)<v_i(b)+v_i(c)$, and let $X$ be an allocation. Then $i$ is safe in $X$ if and only if one of the following holds:
\begin{description}
\item[(T)] $i$ holds $a$, and either $i$ also holds $b$ or $c$, or $b$ and $c$ are not together in a bundle of at least three goods;
\item[(BC)] $i$ holds $b$ and $c$;
\item[(B)] $i$ holds $b$, and $a$ is alone;
\item[(C)] $i$ holds $c$, and $a$ and $b$ are alone;
\item[(E)] $a$, $b$ and $c$ are all alone.
\end{description}
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write $a$, $b$, $c$ also for $v_i(a)$, $v_i(b)$, $v_i(c)$. The sums of subsets of $\{a,b,c\}$ are ordered as $0<c<b<a<b+c<a+c<a+b<a+b+c$: $c<b<a$ by assumption, $a<b+c$ by strict balance, $b+c<a+c$ since $b<a$, $a+c<a+b$ since $c<b$, and $a+b<a+b+c$ since $c>0$. Let $S=X_i\cap R_i$, so $v_i(X_i)=v_i(S)$. For another bundle $X_j$, let $T=X_j\cap R_i$. By Lemma~\ref{lem:threat}, $\theta_i(X_j)=0$ if $T=\emptyset$ or $|X_j|=1$; $\theta_i(X_j)=v_i(T)$ if $X_j$ has a good outside $R_i$; and if $X_j=T$ has two goods, $\theta_i(X_j)$ is the larger value. We go through the possible sets $S$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $S\supseteq\{a,b\}$ or $S\supseteq\{a,c\}$. Then $v_i(S)\ge a+c$, and every other bundle meets $R_i$ in at most one good, so its threat is at most $b<a+c$. Safe; this is (T).
\item $S=\{b,c\}$. Only $a$ lies outside $S$, so every threat is at most $a<b+c$. Safe; this is (BC).
\item $S=\{a\}$. If $b$ and $c$ lie in different bundles, each threat is at most $b<a$. If they lie in the same bundle $X_j$ and $X_j=\{b,c\}$, the threat is $b<a$. If $X_j\supsetneq\{b,c\}$, then $X_j$ contains a good outside $R_i$ (the good $a$ is $i$'s), so $|X_j|\ge3$ and the threat is $b+c>a$. So $i$ is safe if and only if $b$ and $c$ are not together in a bundle of at least three goods: (T).
\item $S=\{b\}$. If $a$ is not alone, its bundle is $\{a,c\}$, with threat $a$, or contains a good outside $R_i$, with threat at least $a$; either way the threat exceeds $b$. If $a$ is alone, its threat is $0$, and the bundle of $c$ has threat at most $c<b$. So $i$ is safe if and only if $a$ is alone: (B).
\item $S=\{c\}$. As before, $a$ must be alone. If $b$ is not alone, its bundle is $\{a,b\}$ (threat $a$) or contains a good outside $R_i$ (threat at least $b$); either way the threat exceeds $c$. If $a$ and $b$ are alone, every threat is $0$. So $i$ is safe if and only if $a$ and $b$ are alone: (C).
\item $S=\emptyset$. Then $v_i(S)=0$, and a bundle with a good of $R_i$ and at least two goods has a positive threat. So $i$ is safe if and only if $a$, $b$ and $c$ are all alone: (E).
\end{itemize}
Conversely, each of (T), (BC), (B), (C), (E) describes one of the safe situations above: (T) is the first and third items; (BC) is $S=\{b,c\}$ or $S=\{a,b,c\}$; (B) forces $a\notin S$, so $S=\{b\}$ with $a$ alone, or $S=\{b,c\}$; (C) forces $S=\{c\}$ with $a$ and $b$ alone; and (E) means that $S$ is empty or a single alone good, which is (T), (B) or (C). \qed
\end{proof}

\begin{proposition}[limits of the shape]\label{prop:neg}
Take values with $v_i(a_i)>v_i(b_i)>v_i(c_i)>0$ and $v_i(a_i)<v_i(b_i)+v_i(c_i)$ for every agent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] Let agents $0$, $1$, $2$ rank $g_0\succ g_1\succ p_i$, where $p_i$ is valued only by agent $i$. No \efxz{} allocation has all bundles of at most two goods, and $\{g_0\},\{g_1\},\{p_0,p_1,p_2\}$, to the three agents in any order, is \efxz{}.
\item[(b)] Let agents $0$, $1$, $2$, $3$ rank $g_0\succ g_2\succ p_0$, $g_0\succ g_2\succ p_1$, $g_1\succ g_2\succ p_2$ and $g_1\succ g_2\succ p_3$, with $p_i$ valued only by agent $i$. Every \efxz{} allocation has bundle sizes $4,1,1,1$, and one exists.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
(a) Five goods in three bundles of at most two goods have sizes $2,2,1$, so exactly one good is alone. Good $g_0$ is everyone's top and has one holder, so two agents do not hold their top: case (T) of Lemma~\ref{lem:L5} fails for them, and cases (C) and (E) need two or three alone goods. So each of these two agents is in case (BC) or (B), and both hold $g_1$, which is impossible. In $\{g_0\},\{g_1\},\{p_0,p_1,p_2\}$, the holder of $g_0$ is in case (T), the holder of $g_1$ in case (B), and the third agent in case (C).

(b) Each of the tops $g_0$ and $g_1$ has an agent ranking it first that does not hold it, a \emph{loser}; the two losers are different agents. A loser is safe only by (BC) or (B), which need it to hold $g_2$, or by (C) or (E), which need $g_2$ alone. The two losers cannot both hold $g_2$, so $g_2$ is alone, and then no agent can be in case (BC), which would put $g_2$ with another good. A loser holding $\{g_2\}$ is in case (B) or (E) and needs its top alone; a loser not holding $g_2$ is in case (C) or (E) and needs its top and $g_2$ alone. So $g_0$, $g_1$ and $g_2$ are alone, held by three different agents, and with at most one bundle of more than two goods the fourth bundle is $\{p_0,p_1,p_2,p_3\}$. Such an allocation exists: $g_0$ to agent~$0$, $g_1$ to agent~$2$, $g_2$ to agent~$1$ and the four goods $p_i$ to agent~$3$ is \efxz{} (cases T, B, T, C). \qed
\end{proof}

\noindent Our script confirms both parts by listing every allocation, for several valuations. In (a) every agent values exactly three goods and is strictly balanced, so it is a core-case instance with no \efxz{} allocation of bundles of at most two goods: the one large bundle of Theorem~\ref{thm:D} cannot be avoided. By (b), its size cannot be bounded by three.

\paragraph{What is proved, and how it was checked.} The Improvement Lemma, the soundness theorem and the correctness of \DE{} are written proofs. They have been checked by computer as described in this section, and they are machine-checked in Lean (the last paragraphs of this section). The Improvement Lemma was found twice, independently: the proof of Section~\ref{sec:improve} (\file{k3/simplify/po/hall/NOTES.md} in~\cite{RepoS}) and the rainbow walk of Section~\ref{sec:second} (\file{k3/simplify/po/potential/NOTES.md}). An independent referee read the first proof and found no error (\file{k3/simplify/po/referee/README.md}). All three, the two provers and the referee, were AI agents: separate sessions of the coding assistant (Claude Code) that helped with this work, the referee given only the written proof. There has been no human peer review. Its four presentation fixes are applied here: the explicit completion and the proof of the owner constraint (Theorem~\ref{thm:sound}); the remark that $h_x$ depends only on $x$ (after Lemma~\ref{lem:forced}); the explicit use of Lemma~\ref{lem:P}(a) when $F=\emptyset$; and the wording of the tests (one completion checked per state). The soundness argument is that of the self-contained proof of K3S (\file{proofs/k3s_standalone.md}, \S5), refereed once with no error, restated for our states.

\paragraph{Three implementations.} The tests are evidence, not proof. In each, every claim of the proof is an assertion, every exchange is re-checked for validity and Pareto domination, and allocations are checked against the raw definition of \efxz{} with exact arithmetic and values $4,3,2$ (for distinct, strictly balanced values \efxz{} depends only on the rankings, by Lemma~\ref{lem:L5}). ``Every profile'' means every assignment of three ranked goods to each agent, up to renaming goods. No test found a failure.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{The first proof as code} (\file{k3/simplify/po/hall/hall.py}): every valid state of every profile with $n=2$ ($m\le6$), $n=3$ ($m\le7$) and $n=4$, $m=5$, that is, $1{,}693{,}750$ valid states, of which the $269{,}028$ that are not completable were each improved as the proof prescribes; $183{,}484$ further states from random profiles ($n\le8$) and cores ($n=5,6$); for each completable state, the completion it built (one per state). Its generator of random states cannot produce states whose need digraph has a cycle; the referee's rejection-sampled states below can; all $774{,}248$ Pareto-optimal valid states of the exhaustive sets and of $3{,}000$ random profiles; and \DE{}'s loop on $44{,}971$ random and core profiles.
\item \emph{The second proof as code} (\file{k3/simplify/po/potential/exchange.py}): about $15.5$ million valid states, from every profile with $n=2$ ($m\le6$), $n=3$ ($m\le7$) and $n=4$ ($m\le6$), $100{,}000$ random profiles with $n\le8$, and samples of cores with $n=5,6$.
\item \emph{The referee's implementation} (\file{k3/simplify/po/referee/}), written from the text of the first proof alone, with random choices wherever the proof leaves one; about $2.9$ million runs: \DE{}'s loop on every profile with $n=2$ ($m\le6$), $n=3$ ($m\le7$) and $n=4$ ($m=5,6$), $2{,}006{,}886$ profiles, on $30{,}700$ core profiles with $n=5$ and on $200{,}000$ random profiles with $n\le9$; $273{,}805$ valid states, every non-completable one improved once ($115{,}304$ improvement runs in all, counted as in its log); soundness on $1{,}130{,}775$ completions (all completions of all valid states with $n\le3$, and of sampled states with $n=4$); $65{,}816$ random valid states sampled by rejection, including states whose need digraph has a cycle; $3{,}000$ rings with up to $9$ free agents and $304$ agents; and peeling by R1 followed by \DE{} on $300{,}000$ instances with real values.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{The script of this paper.} \file{paper/k3-simple/examples/check_examples.py}, a fourth implementation written from this paper, recomputes every example and number of Sections~\ref{sec:intro}, \ref{sec:example}, \ref{sec:size} and~\ref{sec:limits}, cross-checks Section~\ref{sec:example} and the first rows of item~1 with \file{hall.py}, and runs \DE{}, peeling included, on $40{,}000$ random instances: $20{,}000$ with $n\le8$ and $0$ to $3$ valued goods per agent, values in $\{1,\dots,6\}$ (ties, agents with $v(a)\ge v(b)+v(c)$, goods nobody values), and $20{,}000$ in which every agent values three goods and is strictly balanced ($n\le9$). Every output was complete and \efxz{}, with at most one bundle of more than two goods; $10{,}523$ runs needed an exchange, at most $8$. The script exits with status $0$, which it does only if every check passes.

\paragraph{The Lean formalization.} The files \file{lean/EFX/K3DE.lean}, \file{K3DEImprove.lean}, \file{K3DEAlgo.lean} and \file{K3DEExamples.lean} of~\cite{RepoS} (ledger row K3S.PO.LEAN) formalize Sections~\ref{sec:states} and~\ref{sec:improve}, Algorithm~\ref{alg:de} and Theorem~\ref{thm:de} in core Lean~4: no library beyond Lean's own, no unproved step, every declaration on Lean's three standard axioms, all checked by the repository's \file{lean/check.sh}, which also replays the library in a fresh kernel. States are the valid pre-allocations of the previous formalization~\cite{Repo} (an agent holds nothing, one of its goods, or its pair, with needs given by the rankings), and soundness reuses its theorem on completions. The Improvement Lemma is proved for a computable step that follows the loop of Algorithm~\ref{alg:de}. One lemma covers need cycles, pair chains and exchange cycles: a pair chain is closed by an arc from its last agent, which is free, back to the agent that takes its pair. The cycle is the one through $\sigma^n(s)$, where $s$ is the agent $x$ at which (P) fails for a pair chain, and the first agent outside $U$ otherwise; a theorem states that every move is an exchange along such a cycle. The lemma holds for any rankings of three distinct goods per agent; values enter only through soundness. \DE{} itself is a Lean function. The main theorem states that, on every instance with natural-number values in which every agent positively values at most three goods, \DE{} returns an \efxz{} allocation in which all bundles but at most one have at most two goods, after at most $4n$ exchanges. Evaluation by Lean's kernel reproduces the output of Section~\ref{sec:example}. The formal definitions and statements were written by the same AI assistant that helped with this work, following this paper; an independent AI referee session compared them with this text and found no mismatch (\file{k3/simplify/po/referee/lean_audit.md} in~\cite{RepoS}); its documentation fixes are applied.

\paragraph{What is not done.} The running time $O(n(n+m))$ is not formalized (the number of exchanges is), nor are real values (the formalization uses natural numbers; for K3ALG the previous work covers ordered values) or the rainbow-walk proof. The Improvement Lemma has had one referee; Proposition~\ref{prop:short} has had none.

\section{Conclusion and Open Problems}\label{sec:concl}

Every instance in which each agent positively values at most three goods has a complete \efxz{} allocation with at most one bundle of more than two goods, and \DE{} computes one with at most $n$ peeling steps and $4n$ exchanges. The proof rests on one lemma: a valid state that no free agent can complete is Pareto-dominated by a valid state, through a need cycle, a pair chain, or an exchange cycle whose exposure arcs have distinct protecting goods, which a greedy choice of distinct representatives provides.

\emph{Open problems.} (1) \efxz{} with four relevant goods per agent (for ordinary EFX, see~\cite{ViswanathanM24}). With four goods an agent can hold one, two or three of them, and the order of holdings is not determined by the ranking alone, so the utilities of Section~\ref{sec:states} have no direct analogue; one rotation of the K3ALG kind already fails for $n=5$ with one agent valuing four goods~\cite{LinK3,Repo}. (2) Other potentials: the proof works for any potential that grows under Pareto improvements among valid states; minimizing $|\NA|$ alone gave completable states in every test of~\cite{RepoS}, unproved. (3) A better bound than $4n$ exchanges (on random instances at most $8$ were needed; on the rings many more), or a draft that needs none. (4) The least size of the large bundle; Proposition~\ref{prop:neg}(b) shows that three goods do not always suffice. (5) A formalization of the running time of \DE{} and of real values, as was done for K3ALG~\cite{LinK3,Repo}.
